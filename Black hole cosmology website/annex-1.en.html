1.1 - Purpose of the annex

The purpose of this annex is to provide the theoretical framework needed to understand the development of the main work, which is presented in audiovisual format through interviews with professionals in the field of physics and cosmology. Besides contextualizing the debate, the annex sets out the conceptual derivations that gave rise to the questions raised, and these constitute the original theoretical basis of the work. In this sense, the annex gathers both the foundations of current physics and some relevant theoretical frameworks that serve as a starting point for the conceptual derivations behind the questions and reflections developed throughout the work.

1.2 - Criteria for selecting the content

The selection of the models and theories included in this annex reflects their relevance within the current cosmological debate and their capacity to contribute to the conceptual aim of the work. The starting point is the standard cosmological model ΛCDM, since it is the currently most accepted paradigm and the one with the strongest observational support, especially regarding the accelerated expansion of the universe and the large-scale structure of the cosmos. However, this model presents various open questions concerning the origin of the universe, singularities, and the nature of dark energy, which justifies considering complementary theoretical frameworks.

From this starting point, several proposals are integrated that allow possible conceptual extensions of the standard model to be explored: Lee Smolin's cosmological natural selection, different formulations of black hole cosmology, and some relevant contributions by Roger Penrose concerning entropy and the boundary conditions of the universe. The selection of these frameworks is based mainly on their conceptual compatibility, their explanatory capacity in the face of certain cosmological paradoxes, and their internal coherence, rather than on their immediate predictive power, since many of these proposals belong to domains beyond direct experimental verification. This choice does not disregard the importance of empirical verification; rather, it reflects the conceptual nature of the work, which prioritizes internal coherence and the capacity to integrate different theoretical frameworks in order to address fundamental questions that remain open.

1.3 - Relationship with the main body of the work

The main body of this work is developed in audiovisual format through interviews with professionals in the field of physics and cosmology. The questions posed in those interviews stem from the ideas, models, and conceptual derivations set out in this annex. In this sense, the theoretical framework presented here serves as a basis for contextualizing both the topics discussed in the conversations with the experts and the hypothetical questions raised during the debate.

In this way, the annex establishes the definitions, concepts, and theoretical limits needed for the reader to interpret the audiovisual content and the conclusions of the work correctly. The video is therefore not an isolated debate, but the continuation of a prior theoretical reflection set against the opinion of specialized professionals.

1.4 - Internal structure of the annex

To make the theoretical framework easier to follow, the annex is organized into several sections that follow a conceptual progression.

First, some of the foundations of contemporary physics relevant to the cosmological debate are presented, with the aim of establishing the conceptual basis needed to understand the models treated later. Next, several theoretical frameworks that have influenced the development of the ideas analysed in the work are set out. Finally, the conceptual derivations arising from the possible integration of these models are presented — the derivations that formed the basis for the questions put to the professionals interviewed.

This structure makes it possible to distinguish clearly between established knowledge, relevant theoretical proposals, and reflections developed within the work. This in turn makes the debate presented in the main body of the project easier to interpret.

Finally, the main limitations and open questions arising from the proposed framework are collected, with the intention of situating the reflections in a context of uncertainty proper to fundamental research.

2. Established physics

This annex develops a speculative theory based on the foundations of current physics, and follows them until it reaches their logical limits. Before introducing the proposals of this thesis, the framework on which this theory is built must be clearly established — both to give rigour to the derivations presented later, and to allow the reader to identify precisely what is established physics and where the proposals of this thesis begin.

The following sections do not aim to be exhaustive guides to the disciplines treated; they present the concepts, results, and foundations specifically relevant to the derivations developed in section 4.

This theoretical introduction can therefore serve as a review for a reader familiar with general relativity, standard cosmology, and thermodynamics, or, conversely, a less familiar reader will find in it the tools needed to follow the later arguments without requiring additional research.

2.1 - General and special relativity

Relativity is, essentially, a new way of understanding space and time. Before Einstein, physics rested on two absolutes: time passes the same for everyone, and space was simply the empty container in which events occurred. Relativity broke those foundations radically and irreversibly.

Before introducing the theories of relativity, however, a key concept must be established that underpins both and will recur throughout this annex: the concept of the observer.

In physics, an observer is not necessarily a person or a conscious being, but any reference frame from which measurements are made. A camera, a satellite, or a particle detector can act as observers in this sense. What defines an observer is its position, its state of motion, and the set of measurements it can make from where it is.

The question the theories of relativity answer is precisely how one observer's measurements relate to another's. And the answer, counterintuitive as it may seem, is that — like many other things — it depends. Time, length, and even the order in which some events occur can be measured differently by different observers, and both measurements will be equally correct. There is no “superior” observer from which reality is seen objectively. Each observer has their own valid, but partial, description of physical reality.

This idea, which may sound philosophical, has precise and experimentally verified mathematical consequences. It is the starting point of all of relativity, and it will also be one of the central pillars of the annex's proposals developed in section 4.

In 1905, Einstein published his Theory of Special Relativity, establishing two fundamental postulates.

The laws of physics are the same for all observers in uniform motion.

The speed of light in a vacuum is constant regardless of the observer's state of motion.

From these two apparently simple postulates follow consequences that were completely revolutionary for the time: time no longer passes the same for all observers, a moving clock runs slower than one at rest, and space contracts in the direction of motion. Moreover, mass and energy turn out to be equivalent, expressed by the relation:

E = mc²

where E is energy, m is mass, and c is the speed of light. Consequently, nothing with mass can reach the speed of light, since its kinetic energy would approach infinity.

The Theory of General Relativity, published in 1915, went much further. Einstein proposed that gravity is not a force in the Newtonian sense — not an influence acting at a distance between masses; gravity is the curvature of spacetime, caused by the presence of mass and energy. Objects do not fall because an invisible force attracts them, but because they follow the straightest possible path in a curved spacetime. Those paths are called geodesics.

An intuitive way to grasp this concept is to imagine an elastic sheet stretched out horizontally. If we place a heavy ball at its centre, the sheet curves. A smaller ball rolling nearby will not be attracted by any invisible force, but will follow the natural curvature of the surface. Gravity works analogously, but in four dimensions: three spatial and one temporal.

This relationship between mass, energy, and spacetime curvature is expressed by the Einstein field equations:

$$G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu\nu}$$

In simple terms, the left-hand side of the equation describes the geometry of spacetime, and the right-hand side describes its content. Geometry and content determine each other mutually.

This geometric description has consequences that go far beyond everyday gravity.

Gravitational time dilation

From the preceding phenomena we can also derive that the more intense the curvature of spacetime, the more slowly time passes. This effect is expressed as Δt' = Δt √(1 - 2GM/rc²). It is a measurable and real effect: GPS satellites must constantly correct their clocks to account for it.

At extreme measures of curvature, time dilation becomes enormously pronounced. The clear example is near the horizon of a black hole, where time slows dramatically relative to a distant observer. This is reflected in the formula when r approaches the Schwarzschild radius: the factor inside the root approaches zero, and time, from the outside perspective, appears to have stopped completely.

Black holes

If we take the curvature of spacetime to its logical extreme, we arrive at one of the most extraordinary predictions of general relativity: black holes. A black hole is a region of spacetime where the concentration of mass is so great that the resulting curvature prevents anything, not even light, from escaping.

The simplest solution of the Einstein equations describing a black hole was found by Karl Schwarzschild in 1916, shortly after the publication of general relativity. Schwarzschild described a static, spherically symmetric black hole with no electric charge or rotation. Although we would regard it as an idealized case, it contains the fundamental properties defining any black hole, and it is the basis on which more complex models have been built.

The Schwarzschild metric is expressed as ds² = -(1 - 2GM/rc²) c²dt² + (1 - 2GM/rc²)⁻¹ dr² + r²dΩ²

This expression contains two surfaces where the metric behaves singularly, that is, where one of its terms diverges or vanishes. These two surfaces correspond to the event horizon and the central singularity, but their nature is profoundly different. Distinguishing them correctly is essential to understanding both the physics of black holes and the proposals of section 4.

The event horizon

The first notable surface appears at the Schwarzschild radius: r_s = 2GM/c2. At this point the factor 1 - 2GM/rc² vanishes, causing the temporal term of the metric to disappear and the radial term to diverge. Therefore, from outside, an observer would see the time of an object approaching the horizon slow until it formally stops (a direct consequence of the gravitational time dilation described earlier). However, for a free-falling observer crossing the horizon there is no local phenomenon marking the moment of crossing: they experience no infinite force and no abrupt change. The horizon is, locally, a completely regular surface.

This apparent contradiction is resolved by a fundamental concept: the distinction between coordinate singularities and physical singularities. The divergence at r = r_s does not correspond to infinite spacetime curvature (it is an artefact of the Schwarzschild coordinate system, similar to trying to describe the North Pole with latitude and longitude coordinates). If we change to a more suitable coordinate system, such as Eddington-Finkelstein or Kruskal-Szekeres coordinates, the metric remains finite and well defined at the horizon. Spacetime curvature, measured by invariants such as the Kretschmann scalar, does not diverge at this point.

The event horizon is therefore a causal surface, not a physical barrier. What defines it is a global property of spacetime: it is the boundary separating the region from which a signal can still reach a distant observer from the region from which no signal can escape. No local experiment performed by a free-falling observer can detect the exact moment at which they cross the horizon.

The central singularity

At r = 0, by contrast, the situation is radically different. The curvature invariants diverge: the deformation of spacetime grows without limit. This is a physical singularity (not a coordinate artefact) and cannot be eliminated by any change of reference frame. Energy density and curvature become formally infinite, indicating that general relativity ceases to be a valid description in this region.

It is important to understand exactly what it means to say that “the theory ceases to be valid”. It does not mean that its nature becomes infinite, but that the mathematical equations we use to describe it produce results that no longer have any physical meaning. Singularities are, in this sense, the limits of the theory, not the limits of reality. A theory of quantum gravity (one not yet completely formulated) combining general relativity with quantum mechanics is expected to resolve the behaviour of spacetime at distances close to the singularity. For the framework of this work, it is important to retain that the singularity represents a formal limit of the current description, and that the proposals of Chapter 4 will be inserted precisely at that limit.

The commutation of coordinates inside the horizon

Beyond the event horizon, the Schwarzschild metric reveals a notable phenomenon. When r < r_s, the factor (1 - 2GM/rc²) changes sign. The consequence is that the term that was previously temporal acquires a positive sign and the one that was radial acquires a negative sign. In terms of the metric, the coordinate r behaves as a timelike coordinate, and the coordinate t behaves as a spacelike coordinate.

This is not a mathematical abstraction without consequences. The physical implication is direct: inside the horizon, moving towards smaller values of r is not a displacement in space but an advance in time. Just as outside the horizon we cannot avoid advancing in time, inside the horizon we cannot avoid advancing towards r = 0. The singularity is not a place in space, but a moment in the future.

This commutation of roles between coordinates is a direct consequence of standard general relativity and appears in any rigorous treatment of Schwarzschild black holes. It must be said, however, that within the standard framework it is interpreted as a coordinate phenomenon within a continuous spacetime: the interior of the black hole is simply an extension of the same differentiable manifold as the exterior, with the particularity that the “obligatory” direction now points towards r = 0.

This property will be one of the foundations from which Chapter 4 develops an interpretation of black holes as possible origins of independent temporal domains.

Light cones and causal structure

To fully understand the difference between the interior and the exterior of a black hole, we have a powerful conceptual tool: light cones.

In special relativity, a light cone represents the set of all possible directions light can follow from a given point in spacetime. Since nothing can travel faster than light, the light cone defines the limits of causality: any physical trajectory must remain inside the cone or on its surface. An observer's future is confined to the interior of their future light cone.

In a flat spacetime, with no gravity, light cones maintain a uniform orientation. In the presence of mass, however, the curvature of spacetime progressively tilts the light cones in the direction of the gravitational source. As we approach the event horizon, the cones tilt further and further until, exactly at the horizon, one side of the cone points inwards and the other is tangent to the surface. No future trajectory, not even that of light, can point outwards.

Inside the horizon, all light cones point towards r = 0. This is, in fact, another way of seeing the commutation of coordinates: the future of any particle inside the horizon converges inevitably on the singularity. It is not that a force drags it there, but that the very structure of spacetime contains no trajectory pointing in the opposite direction.

This description is formalized in Penrose-Carter diagrams, which represent the complete causal structure of a spacetime — including regions infinitely distant in time and space — within a finite diagram. In a Penrose diagram of a Schwarzschild black hole, the singularity appears as a horizontal line at the top of the diagram, confirming its temporal nature: it is not a point in space, but a surface in the future. These diagrams will be used in Chapter 4 to analyse the proposals of this thesis.

2.2 - Standard cosmology

Standard cosmology is the application of general relativity to the universe as a whole. Starting from the Einstein field equations described in the previous section, and adding certain hypotheses about the large-scale distribution of matter and energy, one arrives at a set of equations describing the evolution of the universe in time.

The cosmological principle and the FLRW metric

The standard cosmological model rests on a simplifying but extraordinarily fruitful hypothesis: the cosmological principle. This principle states that, on sufficiently large scales, the universe is homogeneous (physical properties are the same at every point) and isotropic (there is no privileged direction). This does not mean the universe is uniform on small scales, where there are galaxies, clusters, and voids, but that the distribution of matter and energy, when averaged over distances of hundreds of megaparsecs, is statistically uniform.

Under these conditions, the geometry of spacetime is described by the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) metric: ds² = -c²dt² + a(t)² [dr²/(1-kr²) + r²dΩ²]

The central element is the scale factor a(t), a function depending exclusively on time that encodes how distances between points in the universe change throughout its evolution. If a(t) grows, distances between galaxies increase: the universe expands. If it decreases, the universe contracts. The parameter k describes the global spatial curvature. k = 0 corresponds to a flat universe, k = +1 to a closed universe (spherical geometry), and k = -1 to an open universe (hyperbolic geometry). Current observations, particularly those of the cosmic microwave background, indicate that k is very close to zero: the universe is spatially flat within experimental error margins.

The Friedmann equations

When we substitute the FLRW metric into the Einstein field equations, we obtain the Friedmann equations, which govern the evolution of the scale factor:

(ȧ/a)² = (8πG/3)ρ - kc²/a² + Λc²/3ä/a = -(4πG/3)(ρ + 3p/c²) + Λc²/3

The first equation relates the expansion rate of the universe to its total energy density. The second describes the acceleration or deceleration of that expansion, and depends on both the density and the pressure of the universe's content. The term Λ is the cosmological constant, which Einstein originally introduced to obtain a static universe, but which today is interpreted as a form of energy associated with the vacuum (dark energy).

The quotient ȧ/a appears so frequently that it is given its own name: the Hubble parameter, H(t). Its present value, H₀, determines the current expansion rate of the universe and is one of the most important cosmological constants.

The expansion of the universe and Hubble's law

In 1929, Edwin Hubble observed that distant galaxies recede from us at velocities proportional to their distance:

v = H₀d

It is important to understand correctly what expansion means. It is not that galaxies move through a pre-existing space, but that space itself stretches between them. Two sufficiently distant galaxies can have a mutual recession velocity exceeding the speed of light without violating special relativity, since no object is moving locally at superluminal speed: it is the intervening space that grows.

If the universe is expanding now, the direct logic is that in the past it was smaller and denser. Extrapolating backwards, one arrives at an initial state of extreme density and temperature: the Big Bang. It must be made clear that the Big Bang is not an explosion in a pre-existing space, but the beginning of the expansion of spacetime itself. The question of “what was there before” presupposes a temporal structure that, within the standard framework, does not exist beyond that point. The initial singularity of the Big Bang, like the singularity of black holes, marks a formal limit of general relativity.

The ΛCDM model

The current standard cosmological model is known as ΛCDM, where Λ represents the cosmological constant (associated with dark energy) and CDM stands for Cold Dark Matter. According to the most recent observations, the energy composition of the universe is distributed approximately as follows: 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter, and only 5% ordinary (baryonic) matter.

Dark matter is a type of matter that neither emits, absorbs, nor reflects electromagnetic radiation, but interacts gravitationally. Its existence is deduced from multiple independent observations: galaxy rotation curves, gravitational lensing, and the structure of the anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background. Although its exact nature is unknown, its presence is one of the most solid consensuses of current cosmology.

Dark energy, associated with the cosmological constant Λ, is the dominant component of the universe and the one responsible for its accelerated expansion. In 1998, two independent teams observing type “Ia” supernovae at cosmological distances discovered that the expansion of the universe is not slowing down, as expected from the gravitational attraction of matter, but accelerating. This acceleration requires a component with negative pressure, that is, with an equation-of-state parameter w < -1/3. For a pure cosmological constant, w = -1.

The equation-of-state parameter and the fate of the universe

The equation-of-state parameter w relates the pressure and energy density of a component of the universe through p = wρc². Each type of content in the universe has a characteristic value of w: matter (both baryonic and dark) has w = 0, radiation has w = 1/3, and the cosmological constant has w = -1.

The value of w determines how the energy density of each component evolves as the universe expands. Matter density decreases as a⁻³ (it dilutes with volume), radiation as a⁻⁴ (it dilutes with volume and additionally loses energy through the stretching of its wavelength), and that of the cosmological constant remains constant (it does not dilute). This implies that, in an expanding universe, dark energy ends up dominating over all other components, as we indeed observe today.

The fate of the universe depends on the value of w of dark energy. If w = -1 exactly (pure cosmological constant), the universe will expand exponentially forever, converging towards an empty de Sitter state. If w < -1 (phantom energy), the expansion rate grows without limit up to a final state known as the Big Rip, where expansion tears apart the very structure of spacetime. If w > -1 but close to -1, the behaviour resembles the cosmological-constant case but with subtle differences in long-term evolution. Current observations are consistent with w = -1 within the error margins, but do not rule out slightly different values. This distinction will be relevant in section 4.6, where the annex explores the implications of an emergent w_eff.

The Hubble radius and the cosmological horizon

The expansion of the universe generates a natural scale: the Hubble radius, defined as:

R_H = c/H₀

This distance marks the point beyond which the recession velocity of galaxies exceeds the speed of light. Galaxies beyond the Hubble radius are not observable by conventional means, since no signal emitted from there can reach us. The Hubble radius is not a physical barrier but a causal boundary, analogous in certain respects to the event horizon of a black hole. This analogy is no accident: as we will see in chapter 3, the de Sitter space describing a universe dominated by the cosmological constant formally possesses a cosmological event horizon with thermodynamic properties of its own.

2.3 - Thermodynamics, the evolution of the universe, and the information problem

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics describing the behaviour of systems with many particles in terms of macroscopic quantities such as temperature, pressure, and entropy. Although it was born tied to the heat engines of the nineteenth century, its principles have proved universal: they apply from gases in a laboratory to the evolution of the universe as a whole. For the framework of this work, thermodynamics contributes three fundamental concepts: entropy, the arrow of time, and the final states of matter.

Entropy and the second principle

Entropy is a measure of the number of microscopic configurations (microstates) compatible with an observed macroscopic state. A system with many possible microstates has high entropy; a system with few possible microstates has low entropy. The second principle of thermodynamics states that the entropy of an isolated system can never decrease: natural processes tend towards states of greater disorder.

The statistical formulation of entropy, S = k_B ln Ω , connects a macroscopic quantity (the entropy S) with a microscopic one (the number of microstates Ω). A glass of hot water in a cold room will inevitably cool until it equilibrates with its surroundings, because the number of microstates in which the energy is uniformly distributed is enormously greater than the number in which it is concentrated in the glass. The reverse process violates no fundamental law, but its probability is so extraordinarily low that in practice it never happens.

The arrow of time and the problem of the initial state

The second principle introduces a fundamental asymmetry into physics: while the microscopic laws are time-reversible, macroscopic processes have a preferred direction. That direction, defined by the growth of entropy, is called the thermodynamic arrow of time. Entropy is what gives direction to physical time: without the second law, past and future would be indistinguishable.

This raises a profound question when applied to the universe as a whole: if entropy always grows, the universe must have been in a state of extraordinarily low entropy at its beginning. Indeed, the initial state of the Big Bang has very low entropy, but the reason is less obvious than it might seem.

The Big Bang was a state of extreme temperatures and densities, which might suggest high disorder. However, gravitational entropy works in the opposite way to ordinary thermodynamic intuition: a uniformly distributed universe is a state of low gravitational entropy, because gravity tends to clump matter together. The state of maximum gravitational entropy is not a uniform distribution but an extreme concentration: a black hole.

Roger Penrose has argued extensively that this initial condition of low gravitational entropy is one of the most remarkable and least explained facts of cosmology.

Black hole thermodynamics

In 1972, Jacob Bekenstein proposed that black holes have an entropy proportional to the area of their event horizon, not to their volume:

S_BH = k_B c³ A / 4Għ

This result is notable for several reasons. It connects gravity (G), quantum mechanics (ħ), thermodynamics (k_B), and relativity (c) in a single formula. But above all, it establishes that a black hole's entropy belongs to the horizon, not to the central point. Information about the black hole's content is associated with its boundary, not its interior. This property suggests that the information contained in a region of spacetime could be encoded on its surface.

In 1974, Hawking showed that black holes are not completely black: they emit thermal radiation at a temperature inversely proportional to their mass:

T = ħc³ / 8πGMk_B

This relation has an important consequence: massive black holes are extremely cold (a solar-mass black hole would have a temperature of the order of 10⁻⁸ K, far below that of the cosmic microwave background), while small black holes are hot. Since a black hole emits radiation, it loses mass. As it loses mass, its temperature rises. As the temperature rises, it emits more radiation. The process accelerates progressively until complete evaporation. The evaporation time scales as t ∝ M³, implying that a solar-mass black hole would take approximately 10⁶⁷ years to evaporate — a time enormously longer than the present age of the universe. Nevertheless, on sufficiently long cosmological scales, all black holes evaporate.

The final states of matter

The long-term thermodynamic evolution of the universe follows a sequence leading, step by step, towards states of less structure and greater entropy.

Nuclear fusion inside stars converts light elements into increasingly heavy ones, but the process stops at iron-56, which occupies the minimum of nuclear binding energy. Stars cannot extract more energy from fusion beyond that point, and the most massive ones collapse gravitationally to form black holes. As the universe ages, the remaining baryonic matter cools, decays (on enormous timescales, even protons would decay if their half-life is finite), and gravity concentrates whatever remains into black holes. Finally, Hawking evaporation exhausts the black holes themselves, leaving a universe of thermal radiation of ever-increasing wavelength in an expanding space.

This sequence (fusion up to iron, collapse into black holes, evaporation) describes the complete thermodynamic evolution of matter within a universe dominated by the cosmological constant. Its final state is a thermally dead de Sitter vacuum, without structure, without temperature gradients, and with practically maximal entropy.

The information problem

Hawking radiation generates a profound tension between quantum mechanics and general relativity. In quantum mechanics, the evolution of a system is unitary: information about the initial state is always preserved, though it may be redistributed in extremely complex ways. Hawking showed, however, that the radiation emitted by a black hole is thermal: its distribution depends exclusively on the black hole's temperature and contains no information about the matter that fell in. If the black hole evaporates completely, the original information appears to have vanished from the universe, which violates unitarity.

This tension constitutes the black hole information problem, one of the most important open problems in theoretical physics. One proposal that could resolve it is, among others, the possibility that information escapes gradually, encoded in the Hawking radiation.

2.4 - Quantum mechanics and the uncertainty principle

Quantum mechanics is the theoretical framework describing the behaviour of matter and energy at subatomic scales. Together with general relativity, it constitutes one of the two pillars of modern physics, but the two theories are, to this day, fundamentally incompatible: general relativity describes gravity as a geometric property of a continuous, smooth spacetime, while quantum mechanics describes matter and energy as discrete entities governed by probabilities. The search for a theory unifying the two (a theory of quantum gravity) is one of the central open problems of theoretical physics.

For the framework of this work, we will discuss three essential contributions of quantum mechanics: wave-particle duality, the uncertainty principle, and the notion of quantum state.

Wave-particle duality

One of the most counterintuitive results of quantum mechanics is that subatomic particles behave neither exclusively as point-like objects nor exclusively as waves, but have properties of both kinds depending on the experimental context. An electron can produce interference patterns (wave behaviour) but can also be detected at a specific position (corpuscular behaviour).

This duality is not a limitation of our measuring instruments but a fundamental property of nature. The mathematical description of a quantum particle is a wave function, ψ, encoding all available information about the system. The squared modulus of that function, |ψ|², gives the probability of finding the particle at a given position. The particle is not “at” a specific place until a measurement is made; before the measurement, the wave function describes a superposition of all possible positions.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle

In 1927, Werner Heisenberg established that certain pairs of physical properties cannot be measured simultaneously with arbitrary precision. The best-known formulation relates the position and momentum (mass times velocity) of a particle:

Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2

This inequality is not a technological limitation but a fundamental limit imposed by the very structure of the theory. The more precisely we know a particle's position, the less we can know about its momentum, and vice versa. Analogous uncertainty relations exist for other pairs of variables, such as energy and time:

ΔE · Δt ≥ ħ/2

This second relation has profound implications: it permits temporary fluctuations of energy provided they are sufficiently brief. In the quantum vacuum, particle-antiparticle pairs can appear and disappear spontaneously during intervals short enough for the uncertainty relation to allow it. These vacuum fluctuations are not hypothetical; they have measurable effects, such as the Casimir effect or the Lamb shift.

The Planck scale

The incompatibility between general relativity and quantum mechanics manifests concretely at a particular scale: the Planck scale. Combining the fundamental constants of gravity (G), quantum mechanics (ħ), and relativity (c), one obtains natural units marking the regime where neither theory can function independently:

Planck length: l_P = √(ħG/c³) ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m

Planck time: t_P = √(ħG/c⁵) ≈ 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ s

Planck mass: m_P = √(ħc/G) ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻⁸ kg

At distances below the Planck length or times below the Planck time, the quantum effects of gravity should be dominant, and the classical notions of a continuous spacetime cease to apply. The central singularity of a black hole and the initial singularity of the Big Bang lie precisely in these cases. They are regions where the curvature of spacetime reaches scales at which quantum mechanics and gravity interact inseparably.

This means that any statement about what happens “exactly” at a black hole's singularity, or at the zero instant of the Big Bang, requires a theory of quantum gravity that does not yet exist. This is one of the fundamental boundaries that Chapter 4 will have to respect in its derivations.

Unitarity and quantum evolution

Another relevant concept is the unitarity of quantum evolution. In quantum mechanics, the temporal evolution of a closed system is governed by the Schrödinger equation, which is deterministic and reversible: given the complete quantum state of a system at one instant, the state at any other instant is uniquely determined. Quantum information is never destroyed, though it may be redistributed in ways so complex as to be practically irrecoverable.

This property is what comes into direct tension with the Hawking radiation described in the previous section. If a black hole evaporates completely while emitting purely thermal radiation, the information about the matter that fell in appears to have been lost, which would violate unitarity. Resolving this tension depends, ultimately, on the theory of quantum gravity that would have to correctly describe the behaviour of spacetime at the Planck scale.

3. Established theoretical frameworks

The foundations presented in chapter 2 constitute widely accepted and experimentally verified physics. This chapter introduces a set of theoretical frameworks which, although they do not enjoy the same degree of consensus or empirical verification, represent active and influential lines of research within contemporary cosmology and theoretical physics. Each of these frameworks contributes concepts, tools, or precedents that will be relevant to the derivations of chapter 4.

The aim of this chapter is not to adopt any of these frameworks as valid, but to situate them: to present their contributions, identify their limitations, and establish their relationship with the proposals of this thesis.

3.1 - Gibbons-Hawking radiation

In section 2.3 we presented Hawking radiation as a property of black holes. The event horizon emits thermal radiation at a temperature inversely proportional to the mass. In 1977, Gary Gibbons and Stephen Hawking showed that this phenomenon is not exclusive to black holes, but is a general property of any causal horizon.

The central result is the following: any observer possessing a causal horizon — that is, a boundary beyond which they can receive no signal — will detect thermal radiation at a temperature determined by the geometric properties of that horizon. This applies both to the event horizon of a black hole and to the cosmological horizon of a universe undergoing accelerated expansion.

In the case of a de Sitter universe (a universe dominated exclusively by the cosmological constant Λ), each observer is surrounded by a cosmological horizon at a finite distance. Beyond that horizon, the expansion of space is so rapid that no signal can reach the observer. Gibbons and Hawking showed that this horizon emits thermal radiation at a temperature:

T_GH = ħ√Λ / 2πck_B

This temperature is extraordinarily low (of the order of 10⁻³⁰ K for the observed value of Λ), which makes it undetectable with current technology. Nevertheless, the result is conceptually profound, since it establishes that causal boundaries are not mere mathematical boundaries but real thermodynamic objects, with temperature, entropy, and the capacity to emit radiation.

There is an important difference between a black hole horizon and a cosmological horizon. A black hole horizon is a global property of spacetime: it exists regardless of who observes it. The cosmological horizon of de Sitter space, by contrast, is observer-dependent. Each observer has their own horizon, centred on their position. Two separated observers in a de Sitter space have different horizons and therefore partially incompatible causal histories. This observer dependence will be a central element of section 3.4, where the properties of de Sitter space are developed in detail.

The Gibbons-Hawking result has a direct implication for cosmology: if our universe converges towards a de Sitter state (as the ΛCDM model predicts), then its final state is not a cold, inert vacuum, but a thermally active vacuum, bathed in radiation of constant but minuscule temperature. This distinction, between a “dead” vacuum and a vacuum with thermodynamic properties, will be relevant to understanding the proposals of chapter 4 about the final state of the universe.

3.2 - Black hole complementarity

In 1993, Leonard Susskind, Lárus Thorlacius, and John Uglum proposed a principle addressing one of the deepest tensions in black hole physics: complementarity. The problem they attempt to resolve is the following: when an object falls into a black hole, what happens to it? From the perspective of a free-falling observer, the object crosses the event horizon with no local incident and continues towards the singularity. From the perspective of an external observer, by contrast, the object never actually crosses the horizon: gravitational time dilation makes the crossing take a formally infinite time, and the object's information is encoded in the Hawking radiation emitted by the horizon.

These two descriptions appear contradictory: the object cannot be simultaneously inside and outside the black hole. The complementarity principle resolves this tension with a causal argument: there is no contradiction because no observer can access both descriptions at once. The observer falling into the black hole cannot communicate their experience to the external observer (since no signal can escape the horizon), and the external observer cannot verify what happens inside. The two descriptions are complete and internally consistent, but causally inaccessible to each other.

It should be noted that complementarity does not resolve the information problem described in section 2.3. It does not explain how information leaves the black hole, nor whether it is preserved or lost. What it does is something different: it establishes that a single globally consistent description of spacetime is not required. Two apparently incompatible descriptions can legitimately coexist if no physical observer can access both.

This principle is a direct precedent for the proposals of this thesis. The idea that a causal boundary admits multiple, equally valid descriptions, with no observer able to compare them directly, is exactly the kind of structure that chapter 4 applies to the boundaries of temporal islands and to the duality between singularities.

3.3 - Lee Smolin's cosmological natural selection

In 1992, Lee Smolin proposed a hypothesis connecting black hole physics with cosmology in a radically new way: every black hole forming in our universe generates, in its own interior, a new child universe. That child universe inherits the physical constants of the parent, but with small random variations, analogous to biological mutations. The result is a network of universes subject to a process of natural selection: universes producing more black holes leave more descendants, and therefore the physical constants favouring black hole formation become statistically dominant in the population of universes.

Smolin's hypothesis has an important virtue: it generates testable predictions. If cosmological natural selection is correct, the physical constants of our universe should be optimized to maximize black hole production. Any small variation of the constants should produce fewer black holes, not more. Smolin has argued that this is compatible with observations, although the question remains open and debated.

The main challenges of the proposal are significant. First, the mutation mechanism is unspecified: Smolin argues that the constants vary slightly, but does not derive that variation from any concrete physical process. Second, it is not clear how the constants are transmitted from parent to child, nor what process connects gravitational collapse with the start of a new expansion. Moreover, empirical verification is inherently difficult, since we have no observational access to the child universes.

Cosmological natural selection is an important structural precedent for the present work, in that it establishes the idea that black holes could be the origins of child universes. However, the annex arrives at it by a different path. Smolin posits the network of universes as a fundamental hypothesis and adds the mechanism of mutation and selection to it. This thesis, by contrast, does not posit the network but derives it as a consequence of standard ΛCDM cosmology, without needing mutation or selection to establish its structure. This distinction is fundamental and is developed in section 4.7.

3.4 - The holographic principle

The holographic principle is a conjecture arising from black hole thermodynamics with profound implications for the nature of spacetime. Its general formulation states that the information contained in a volume of spacetime can be completely described by a theory defined on its boundary surface.

The origin of the principle lies in the Bekenstein-Hawking formula presented in section 2.3, which states that a black hole's entropy is proportional to the area of its event horizon, not to its volume. Bekenstein further showed that a black hole's entropy is the maximum entropy any region of spacetime with the same boundary area can contain. This means that the total number of degrees of freedom of a region does not scale with volume, which would be the more intuitive option, but with the area of its surface.

Gerard 't Hooft proposed in 1993 that this property was not exclusive to black holes but a fundamental principle of quantum gravity. Leonard Susskind developed the idea and formalized it as the holographic principle: all the physics occurring within a volume can be described by an equivalent theory living on the boundary of that volume, with an information density of one bit per Planck area.

The best concrete evidence for the holographic principle comes from the AdS/CFT correspondence, proposed by Juan Maldacena in 1997. This correspondence establishes an exact equivalence between a theory of gravity in an anti-de Sitter (AdS) spacetime and a conformal field theory (CFT) defined on the boundary of that spacetime. The boundary theory contains no gravity, but reproduces exactly all the results of the gravitational theory of the interior. Although the AdS/CFT correspondence is formulated for spacetimes with a negative cosmological constant (and our universe has Λ > 0), the result strongly suggests that holographic encoding of information is a general property of gravity.

For the present work, the holographic principle is relevant because it establishes a theoretical precedent: if the information of a volume is encoded on its boundary surface, then the information of a child universe could be encoded on the event horizon of the parent black hole.

3.5 - Expansion and “de Sitter” space

De Sitter space is the solution of the Einstein equations for an empty universe with a positive cosmological constant (Λ > 0). Although it is an idealization (our universe contains matter and radiation), de Sitter space describes the state towards which any universe dominated by the cosmological constant converges. As expansion dilutes matter and radiation, Λ becomes the dominant component and the geometry of spacetime approaches the pure de Sitter solution asymptotically. Within the ΛCDM model, this is the future of our universe.

De Sitter space has a causal structure with remarkable properties. In a de Sitter universe, expansion is exponential, and therefore the scale factor grows as a(t) ∝ e^(Ht)

This expansion generates a cosmological horizon around each observer, at a distance r_c = c/H. Beyond that distance, the expansion of space exceeds the speed of light and no signal can reach the observer.

Unlike the event horizon of a black hole, which is a global property of spacetime, the cosmological horizon of de Sitter space is observer-dependent. Each observer has their own horizon, centred on their position. Two observers at nearby distances share practically the same horizon, but two sufficiently separated observers have horizons that do not completely overlap. Therefore one can receive signals the other cannot, and their causal histories are partially incompatible.

As seen in section 3.1, the cosmological horizon of de Sitter space emits Gibbons-Hawking radiation at a temperature T_GH = ħ√Λ / 2πck_B. That temperature, minuscule though it is, establishes that de Sitter space is not an inert vacuum but a thermodynamic system with a finite entropy associated with its horizon. The entropy of the cosmological horizon follows the same Bekenstein-Hawking relation as that of black holes: it is proportional to the area of the horizon.

De Sitter space therefore presents a combination of properties that make it conceptually rich. It is a state of thermodynamic equilibrium (constant temperature, maximum entropy for the observer), but at the same time a state of accelerated expansion. The observer cannot access information beyond their horizon, in the same way that an external observer cannot access the interior of a black hole. This analogy between the cosmological horizon and a black hole horizon is not superficial: both are causal boundaries with thermodynamic properties, and their physics is described with the same mathematical tools.

3.6 - Black hole cosmologies

The idea that the interior of a black hole could give rise to a child universe is by no means exclusive to the present work or to Smolin's proposal. There is an active, if minority, field of research exploring various variants of this hypothesis under the general name of Black Hole Cosmologies.

The most developed proposal within this field is that of Nikodem Popławski, which starts from Einstein-Cartan theory, an extension of general relativity incorporating spacetime torsion as an additional dynamical variable. In the standard theory, spacetime has curvature but no torsion. Einstein-Cartan theory adds torsion as a response to the spin of fermionic matter. The main consequence for cosmology is that, at extreme densities close to the singularity, torsion generates a repulsive pressure preventing complete collapse. Instead of reaching a singularity, matter bounces and begins an expansion inside the event horizon, giving rise to a new spacetime domain which, from within, resembles an expanding universe.

Popławski's model has the virtue of providing a concrete physical mechanism for avoiding the singularity and generating the expansion. However, it requires modifying the underlying theory of gravity, since torsion is not a property of standard general relativity but a theoretical extension which, while consistent, has not been experimentally verified.

Other variants of black hole cosmologies exist that explore alternative mechanisms: quantum bounces derived from Loop Quantum Gravity, phase transitions of spacetime at Planck densities, or constructions based on cosmic strings. All share the general structure (collapse inside a black hole generates a child universe), but they differ in the mechanism connecting collapse with expansion.

The fundamental distinction between those proposals and those of this annex is the following: the existing models posit a specific mechanism (torsion, quantum bounce, phase transition) and construct it within a modified or extended theory. This thesis, by contrast, neither modifies general relativity nor posits any additional mechanism. Its strategy is different: it starts from the consequences of the standard ΛCDM model and derives the network of universes from them as a logical result, identifying gravitational collapse and the rupture of the manifold as the connecting mechanism, without adding new ingredients to the underlying physics.

3.7 - Penrose's Conformal Cyclic Cosmology

In 2010, Roger Penrose proposed a cosmological model radically different from the preceding ones: Conformal Cyclic Cosmology (CCC). The central argument starts from an observation about the nature of the universe's remote future and its geometric relationship with the Big Bang.

In a universe dominated by the cosmological constant, the final state is a de Sitter space in which all massive matter has disappeared. The stars have gone out, the black holes have evaporated, and only photons and other massless particles remain. For massless particles, scale has no physical meaning: a photon has no internal clock, does not experience the passage of time, and its physics is invariant under conformal rescalings (transformations that change distances but preserve angles). This means that, for the only inhabitants of the remote future, an infinitely expanded universe is geometrically indistinguishable from an infinitely compressed one.

Penrose proposes that this conformal equivalence is not a mere mathematical curiosity but a real physical identification. The remote future of one aeon (a complete cycle of the universe) is literally the Big Bang of the next aeon. The transition from aeon to aeon requires no singularity and no bounce mechanism: it is a smooth conformal rescaling connecting a state of maximum dispersion with a state of maximum compression.

Penrose's argument about entropy is subtle. At both extremes (remote future and Big Bang), gravitational entropy is zero or undefined: in the remote future because there is no massive matter that could collapse gravitationally, and at the Big Bang because the distribution is uniform (which, as seen in section 2.3, is a state of low gravitational entropy). CCC proposes that the entropy counter resets at each conformal transition, allowing each aeon to begin with low entropy without violating the second principle within any individual aeon.

CCC has attracted interest for its elegance and for the observational predictions derived from it. Penrose has argued that collisions between supermassive black holes in a previous aeon would leave circular imprints in the cosmic microwave background of the following aeon (the so-called “Hawking circles”). The observational evidence for these imprints, however, is contested, and there is no consensus about their detection.

The limitations of CCC are important to situate. The conformal rescaling connecting the remote future with the Big Bang is a mathematical identification, not a causal physical process. There is no mechanism dynamically connecting the two states. The transition is a boundary condition imposed on the geometry, not an evolution governed by equations of motion. Moreover, CCC requires all massive matter to disappear in the remote future, which depends on hypotheses about proton decay and the complete evaporation of all black holes.

4. Derivations and hypotheses

The preceding sections have established the framework on which this chapter is built. Section 2 presented the foundations of established physics (general relativity, standard cosmology, thermodynamics, and quantum mechanics) that serve as the starting point. Section 3 contextualized the existing theoretical frameworks that the annex takes as precedents or as points of contrast. With these tools, we have the elements needed to follow what comes next.

This chapter is the core of the work. It presents the original proposals of this thesis: logical derivations from the established foundations, extensions of existing theoretical frameworks, and new arguments that follow from taking standard physics to its own limits. None of the proposals that follow is presented as established physics. Each is explicitly identified as an original derivation, a speculative interpretation, or a working hypothesis, depending on its degree of grounding.

The chapter's structure follows a deliberate progression. The first sections, 4.1 to 4.5, establish the framework's fundamental concepts: orthogonal arrows of time, the de Sitter derivations, temporal islands, entropy at singularities, and the duality of contrary singularities. The middle sections, 4.6 to 4.8, treat the cosmological consequences of those concepts: terminal de Sitter, the cosmological network of black holes, and entropic accounting between universes. The final sections, 4.9 to 4.12, develop the physical mechanism that makes the theory possible: dimensional genesis, the temporal parenthesis model, the observational signatures, and the unification of all the arguments into a single causal thread.

Each section builds on the previous ones. The connections between sections are indicated explicitly so that the line of argument can be followed without losing sight of the overall structure. The limitations of each argument are identified as they arise, and are developed in detail in chapter 5.

A note on the epistemological status of the chapter as a whole. The framework presented here is logically coherent and internally consistent. It starts from well-grounded premises and follows them rigorously. Nevertheless, it is a speculative framework: its central proposals (the rupture of the manifold, dimensional genesis, genuine temporal orthogonality) cannot be demonstrated within the existing formalism and require a theory of quantum gravity to be formalized. The annex does not claim to be a complete physical theory. It aims to be a rigorous conceptual framework that identifies precisely where current physics is incomplete and in which direction a complete theory would have to point.

4.1 - Orthogonal arrows of time

In section 2.1 we presented the commutation of Schwarzschild coordinates inside the event horizon: when r < r_s, the factor (1 - 2GM/rc²) changes sign, the radial coordinate r acquires timelike character and the coordinate t acquires spacelike character. This is a direct result of standard general relativity. Within the conventional framework it is interpreted as a coordinate phenomenon inside a continuous spacetime: the black hole's interior is an extension of the same differentiable manifold as the exterior, with the particularity that the obligatory direction of advance now points towards r = 0.

The annex proposes an alternative interpretation of this result. The central claim is the following: the commutation of coordinates is not an artefact within a continuous spacetime, but the expression of a fundamental independence between the interior's temporal axis and the exterior's temporal axis. From the moment an observer crosses the event horizon, their obligatory temporal advance no longer points in the same direction as exterior time. Their temporal axis has decoupled from the parent's time.

It is necessary to be completely transparent about the epistemological status of this proposal, and that requires a twofold clarification.

The first concerns physical content. In standard general relativity, the commutation of coordinates is a well-understood phenomenon within a continuous manifold: it does not imply that the interior constitutes an “independent temporal axis”, but that the character of the coordinates changes on crossing the horizon. The interpretation expressed in this annex — that this change of character expresses a genuine temporal independence between two causally disconnected domains — does not follow from the formalism of general relativity. It is a conceptual proposal requiring new physics to be formulated rigorously.

The second is geometric in nature. When we speak of “orthogonality” between temporal axes, it must be understood that the term is not used in the technical sense of Lorentzian geometry. In a spacetime with signature (−,+,+,+), two timelike vectors cannot be orthogonal: their Lorentzian scalar product is always negative, never zero. Lorentzian orthogonality is defined only between a timelike and a spacelike vector, or between two spacelike vectors. The notion of “temporal orthogonality” proposed here therefore does not exist within the standard formalism. It is used as a concept describing the relationship between two temporal domains that have lost all causal connection, but its mathematical formalization belongs to the new physics the framework requires and which does not exist today.

An analogy may help make the idea intuitive, provided it is understood as strictly pedagogical. Imagine a right triangle where the base represents the time of an observer at rest and the hypotenuse represents the time of an observer in relative motion. The angle between base and hypotenuse would indicate, figuratively, the temporal difference between the two observers. At everyday velocities the angle would be imperceptible, but at significant fractions of the speed of light it would become noticeable. What is proposed is that, at the horizon of a black hole, this “angle” reaches ninety degrees.

This analogy is not geometrically rigorous: the geometry of spacetime is Lorentzian, not Euclidean, and there is no continuous progression within the standard formalism leading from hyperbolic tilt to Euclidean perpendicularity. This image serves only to introduce the idea. The complete argument leading to orthogonality, including its grounding in the commutation of the metric signature and the rejection of the alternatives (instant-by-instant correspondence and a variable dilation coefficient), is developed in section 4.10, where the temporal parenthesis model also shows its consequences.

What, then, does the proposal imply if we accept it as a working framework? If the temporal axis of a black hole's interior is independent of exterior time, then the interior is not a continuation of the parent's time, but a temporal domain of its own opening in a direction the parent cannot traverse. The parent universe's timeline cannot continue until that independent axis has completed its course. The child must reach its own final state, its own de Sitter space, for the temporal parenthesis to close and the original timeline to resume. It is not that the parent's time stops: it is that the child exists in a temporal direction the parent does not contain.

It is worth specifying what this principle does not claim. It does not claim that time does not exist outside causal boundaries. It claims that the time of one causal domain is neither an extension nor a continuation of the time of another causal domain when the two are separated by an event horizon. Nor should it be confused with the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics: there are no branches of reality diverging. What is proposed is that the same physical reality, described from two causally separated positions, produces temporal axes that cannot be reconciled by any transformation within the existing framework.

4.2 - Derivations from de Sitter space

This section takes the established physics of de Sitter space presented in section 3.4 and derives from it the consequences that standard cosmology acknowledges but does not fully develop. The aim is to show that the ΛCDM model, taken to its own limits, produces a causal structure with implications going beyond what the standard framework usually makes explicit.

Gravitational binding in the presence of Λ

The starting point is the criterion of gravitational binding. Two systems are gravitationally bound if their total mechanical energy is negative, that is, if their mutual gravity is sufficient to hold them together against any perturbation. In the presence of the cosmological constant, this criterion acquires a natural scale: there exists a critical radius r_c defined by:

GM/r_c³ = Λc²/3 which can be rewritten as: r_c = (3GM/Λc²)^(1/3)

Within this radius, the system's own gravity dominates over the expansive effect of Λ. Outside it, expansion dominates over gravity. Up to this point it is standard physics.

A gravitationally bound system moves as a coherent unit, and therefore the decision as to whether it is bound is taken for the system as a whole, not for each part separately. Within a bound system, the effect of Λ is not exactly zero, but it is negligible compared with local gravity: the curvature due to mass completely dominates the cosmological term for any realistic astronomical mass.

The distinction between r_c and the de Sitter horizon

The argument that follows requires a distinction that must be established precisely: r_c and the de Sitter horizon r_dS are two qualitatively different quantities.

The radius r_c is a radius of dynamical binding. It determines whether a system is gravitationally bound at a given instant. It is not a causal horizon. Exceeding r_c means that expansion dominates over gravity, but it implies no immediate causal disconnection: systems exceeding r_c can continue interacting causally for a long time.

The de Sitter horizon, by contrast, is the distance beyond which the recession velocity exceeds c and no future signal can arrive from outside:

r_dS = √(3/Λ)

This is an absolute and permanent causal boundary. For realistic astronomical masses (a galaxy cluster of M ~ 10¹⁴ M☉ with Λ ~ 10⁻⁵² m⁻²), r_c is of the order of megaparsecs while r_dS is of the order of gigaparsecs. The difference is three orders of magnitude.

The consequence of this distinction is the following. When an unbound system exceeds r_c, expansion begins to dominate and the system progressively disperses. But permanent causal disconnection does not occur until the separation between systems exceeds r_dS. During the intermediate period, which in cosmological terms can be enormously long, the systems can continue interacting causally even though they are not gravitationally bound. What is irreversible and permanent once r_dS is reached is causal disconnection: no signal can ever connect the two systems again, and no future evolution can reverse that situation.

Permanent causal separation as an ontological property

The original derivative step in this annex is to take permanent causal separation beyond r_dS seriously as an ontological claim. It is not simply that two systems cannot interact. It is that accessible physical reality — the set of all events with which a system can interact — is fundamentally and permanently different for each of them.

What is causally real for one system may be causally non-existent for another, without either being mistaken. Two systems separated by r_dS do not simply measure each other differently: each exists in a causal domain from which the other is permanently absent.

The status of this claim must be specified. That two systems separated by r_dS cannot interact is established physics. That this constitutes an ontological difference (and not merely a descriptive one) is an interpretation of our own. Standard cosmology does not pronounce on whether permanent causal separation implies a different reality for each system or simply a practical impossibility of communication. For the purposes of this annex we adopt the first reading, and the sections that follow depend on it.

The collapse of temporal ordering between causal domains

The deepest consequence of permanent causal separation is that the temporal ordering between events in causally separated domains breaks down. In standard relativity, two observers may disagree about the simultaneity of events, but not about their causal ordering: if A causes B, all observers will agree that A comes before B. In domains separated by r_dS, however, no signal can connect the two domains. The question “which of the two events happened first” has no consistent answer, because temporal ordering is a relational property requiring causal connectivity in order to exist.

This is not a technological problem of measurement; it is a direct consequence of the causal structure of de Sitter space. If no signal can connect two events, no clock can order them. A real tension within ΛCDM is identified here: if temporal ordering breaks down between causally separated domains, and if gravitational binding decisions depend on events occurring in sequence, there are scenarios in which the model cannot produce a consistent answer. This tension is developed formally in section 5.1 as the three-body paradox.

The cosmological horizon as a thermodynamic boundary

From the Gibbons-Hawking radiation presented in section 3.1, we can derive that the cosmological horizon of de Sitter space is not merely a geometric boundary but a real thermodynamic object. It has a temperature (T_GH = ħ√Λ / 2πck_B), an entropy proportional to its area, and it emits thermal radiation. The final state of the universe under ΛCDM is not a dead vacuum but a thermally active one, bathed in constant radiation of minuscule but real temperature.

4.3 - Temporal islands and derealization

This section builds the concept of the temporal island as the fundamental unit of the BHC framework. A temporal island is a gravitationally bound system defining the only domain where time is operationally defined, where causality is accessible, and where physical reality has measurable content. Outside a temporal island, time is not merely difficult to measure: it is operationally absent. Derealization is the process by which the constituents of a temporal island lose their causal future within it and enter a state of permanent causal inertia.

Grounding derealization

Derealization is not a claim about the metaphysical existence of a particle; it is a claim about its physical state. A particle exceeding r_dS is physically inert permanently and irreversibly. It cannot interact with any other system for the rest of spacetime's existence. This is a direct consequence of the causal structure of de Sitter space, not a philosophical position.

What this annex will call derealization is precisely that state: permanent causal inertia. It implies no destruction, no disappearance, and requires no claim about existence in an absolute sense. It implies only that the constituent has irreversibly lost all capacity for future interaction.

Three perspectives ground this concept.

The first is the causal structure of de Sitter space. In a de Sitter space, once two systems exceed r_dS, no signal can ever connect them again. This is established physics. The direct consequence is that any physical process involving interaction between the two systems is impossible for the rest of spacetime's existence. It is not a technological or practical limitation. It is a property of the causal structure. A particle that has exceeded r_dS with respect to all other existing systems is in a state of complete and permanent causal isolation. No future evolution of any system can reverse that situation.

The second is the entropic consequence of that isolation. A particle in complete causal isolation is in a state where all its possible internal configurations produce the same observable result for any external system: complete causal absence. No internal configuration is distinguishable from any other from outside, because no configuration can produce any external effect. By the statistical definition of entropy S = k_B ln(Ω), the number of externally distinguishable microstates is Ω_external = 1, and therefore S_external = k_B ln(1) = 0. It is necessary to be precise about what this claim means. Saying the entropy is 0 is an epistemic claim about external accessibility, not an ontological claim about the system's internal entropy. The particle's internal entropy does not disappear. It continues to exist. What disappears is the possibility of any external observer accessing it.

The third is the quantum-mechanical perspective. A causally isolated particle has a wave function that continues to evolve unitarily. This is correct and is not denied. The particle does not disappear in any quantum sense: its degrees of freedom persist mathematically. What is claimed, and what quantum mechanics does not contradict, is that none of those degrees of freedom can produce any external effect for the rest of spacetime's existence. The distinction is between mathematical existence, which persists, and physical relevance, which has been permanently lost. We will therefore use the term “derealization” to refer exclusively to the loss of physical relevance, not to mathematical disappearance.

Formal definition of the temporal island

A temporal island T is a gravitationally bound system of total mass M(t), with critical radius r_c(t) = (3GM(t)/Λc²)^(1/3), within which three conditions hold simultaneously.

The first is causal connectivity. All constituents share a common causal future and interaction between any pair of constituents is in principle possible. The second is operational time: there exists a shared temporal structure within the system, where time is definable relationally through the interactions between constituents. The third is causal reality: the constituents of the temporal island can cause measurable effects within the system and are not in a state of permanent causal inertia.

The radius r_c(t) is not a fixed boundary. It depends on M(t), which is a decreasing function of time for the reasons developed in section 4.6. As M(t) decreases, r_c(t) contracts. Constituents that were inside the temporal island may progressively find themselves outside the new r_c. When that happens, the constituent has lost its causal future within T. It cannot return. It enters the process of derealization described in the previous section.

The distinction between r_c and r_dS must be maintained in the context of temporal islands, because they operate at different levels.

The radius r_c is the dynamical boundary of the gravitational binding of a specific temporal island T. It determines whether a constituent is part of T at a given instant: if it is inside r_c, it belongs to the bound system; if it has exceeded it, it no longer does. That unbinding, however, is not necessarily permanent. A constituent that has left T's r_c could, in principle, be captured by another gravitationally bound system forming later, provided it remains within a causally connected domain.

The radius r_dS, by contrast, is the absolute causal boundary of the global de Sitter space. When a constituent exceeds r_dS, it enters a causal domain permanently separated from everything: no future evolution can reconnect it with any other system. This is permanent and absolute derealization, which cannot be reversed by any future formation of temporal islands.

The relationship between the two radii is therefore hierarchical: r_c determines membership of a particular temporal island at a particular moment, while r_dS determines the global causal accessibility of the universe. A constituent may have exceeded its original temporal island's r_c and find itself in an unbound region, yet still within the global r_dS; in that case it still belongs to the same causal domain and could, in principle, participate in the formation of new structures. Only when it exceeds r_dS does its derealization become definitive.

Representational singularity at r_c

The formal treatment of bound systems as point masses leads to a precise internal tension at r_c. That tension constitutes what we will call a representational singularity.

The critical radius r_c is derived by treating the system as a point mass M located at the centre of mass. That idealization is valid for describing the system's gravitational interaction with external objects at large distances. But r_c is precisely the distance at which we ask whether a constituent is part of the system or not. Here the tension appears: one cannot use the point-mass idealization to define a boundary and simultaneously use that boundary to ask where the point is. The boundary was derived assuming the system is a point. Asking whether a constituent located exactly at r_c is inside or outside the system is asking whether the point is inside or outside itself. The model has no consistent answer.

The status of this claim must be made explicit. The radius r_c is not a sharp boundary in standard cosmology: it is a characteristic scale, an estimate of the distance at which gravitational and expansive effects are comparable. It is not claimed that there is a physical singularity at that point, but that the point-mass idealization, when used to derive r_c and simultaneously to evaluate constituents at that boundary, produces an internal self-contradiction. This is a singularity in the description, not in the underlying physics. The representational singularity is not a place in spacetime where anything singular happens physically. It is the point at which our mathematical description of bound systems ceases to be internally consistent.

Equivalence between temporal islands and universes

In this annex we will define a universe as a causally autonomous temporal domain, bounded by a process leading some physical quantity to an operationally infinite limit — whether infinite compression or infinite expansion — beyond which the interior temporal sequence cannot continue.

Under this definition, the difference between a temporal island and a universe is exclusively the type of boundary delimiting them. Temporal islands are bounded by r_c and eventually by r_dS. Universes in the BHC framework are bounded by the event horizon of a black hole (the product of infinite compression) or by the de Sitter cosmological horizon (the product of infinite expansion). Both types of boundary produce the same operational result: the termination of the temporal sequence for interior constituents, the impossibility of causal contact with the exterior, and thermal radiation from the boundary via the Gibbons-Hawking mechanism.

It must be made explicit what this equivalence does not claim. It does not claim that a galaxy cluster is a universe in the standard cosmological sense, where “universe” means the totality of spacetime or a causally connected region on the largest scale. It claims that under the annex's operational definition, based on causality and operational time, the two objects share the same fundamental structure.

The standard distinction between “universe” and “gravitationally bound system” is valid and important in standard cosmology. This annex does not deny it. What it proposes is that, at the extreme limits where causal boundaries become absolute (when r_dS becomes the relevant boundary), the distinction loses operational content. At that limit, the bound system and the universe are indistinguishable in every physically meaningful sense. Every gravitationally bound system reaching complete causal isolation is a universe in the only operationally meaningful sense at the extreme limit. The difference we colloquially draw between “our universe” and “a galaxy” is not categorical: it is geometric, determined by which type of causal boundary delimits each system and which regime we find ourselves in.

Hierarchy of singularities

The annex identifies three levels of singularity coexisting within the ΛCDM framework, each of a different nature but related by a structure of inclusion. Each level contains the lower ones and determines their physical relevance.

The first level consists of the representational singularities at r_c, described in section 4.3.3. They arise when the point-mass idealization becomes self-contradictory at its own boundary. They are local, they scale with M(t), and they exist for every gravitationally bound system. They imply neither infinite curvature nor permanent causal rupture. They imply a collapse of the system's mathematical description when applied to its own limits.

The second level consists of curvature singularities: the classical singularities of general relativity, such as the centre of black holes and the initial singularity of the Big Bang. They occur where the curvature of spacetime diverges and the Einstein field equations cease to produce physically meaningful results. They are contained within temporal islands: a black hole is an object inside a bound system.

The third level is the causal limit at the de Sitter scale. Terminology must be precise here: de Sitter space is not a singularity in the technical sense of general relativity, since its geometry is smooth and geodesically complete. We will use “singularity” in a specific operational sense: a state where the temporal sequence has lost content, where no interactions are possible, and where the existence of any constituent is in a state of permanent causal inertia. It is a singularity in the operational description of time, not in the geometry of spacetime. It contains all the previous levels in the sense that the final de Sitter state is the result of the complete evolution of every bound system and every black hole that has existed.

The structure of inclusion is therefore: Level 1 ⊂ Level 2 ⊂ Level 3, in the sense that each higher level contains the lower ones and determines whether the singularities of the lower levels are physically relevant to the general system. A curvature singularity inside a black hole that has been derealized with respect to its temporal island is a curvature singularity physically irrelevant to the parent system: it exists mathematically, but has no accessible causal future within the system containing it.

This hierarchy is a conceptual instrument and a precedent of vocabulary, not a formalized theorem. Formalizing the relationships between the three levels within a consistent mathematical framework requires quantum gravity. What the annex establishes is that the three levels exist, that they differ in nature, and that their interaction generates conceptual tensions which chapter 5 explores.

4.4 - Entropy at singularities

This section examines the entropic and probabilistic properties of the singularities identified in the hierarchy of section 4.3.5. The aim is twofold: first, to establish why classical entropy is zero or undefined at singularities; second, to propose an interpretation based on observer dependence. The conclusion proposed is that both entropy and probability, in certain extreme limits, could be understood as relational properties of the causal framework rather than absolute properties of matter. This is a working hypothesis of the annex, not an established derivation.

Entropy at classical singularities

The first question is: what is the entropic state of a curvature singularity, whether the centre of a black hole or the initial singularity of the Big Bang?

The established answer is that black holes have an enormous entropy, given by the Bekenstein-Hawking formula presented in section 2.3: S_BH = k_B c³ A / 4Għ

It is necessary to be precise about where that entropy resides. The Bekenstein-Hawking formula assigns it to the event horizon, not to the internal singularity. A black hole's entropy is a property of its causal boundary, not of the central point where curvature diverges. This distinction, which may seem minor, is fundamental to the argument of this section.

When we try to apply statistical mechanics directly to the point of the singularity, treating it as a physical object, the Einstein equations cease to be valid. The density is formally infinite. The microstates are not well defined because the theoretical framework defining them has stopped working. Therefore, the entropy at the classical singularity is neither zero nor infinite: it is undefined. This is not a physical fact about the singularity, but the honest result of a theory pushed beyond its domain of validity. The singularity is not an object with defined entropic properties: it is the limit at which the tools of description stop working.

A future theory of quantum gravity is expected to replace the singularity with a finite, regular structure (a “fuzzball” in the string theory framework, or a structure derived from loop quantum gravity, for example), where entropy would again have precise physical content. Until that theory exists, claims about the exact entropy at the singularity are provisional.

Impossibility of normalization with infinite microstates

This subsection sets out a mathematical fact relevant to the discussion of singularities, but one that must be interpreted correctly.

The problem is the following. In probability theory, a uniform distribution over an infinite discrete set is not normalizable. Probability theory requires that the sum of all the probabilities of a system's microstates be exactly 1 (the normalization condition). For an infinite discrete set with a uniform distribution, there are two possibilities and neither works. If each microstate has probability p > 0, the sum of all probabilities diverges to infinity. If p = 0 for each microstate, the sum is zero. There is no value of p allowing the sum to be exactly 1. This is an exact mathematical property, not an approximation.

Physics avoids this problem not by denying it, but by ensuring that its premises do not apply to observable systems. Wave functions in quantum mechanics decay at infinity and are therefore normalizable. Boltzmann weights in statistical mechanics exponentially favour low-energy states, concentrating the probability measure rather than distributing it uniformly. All known physical systems use non-uniform distributions permitting normalization.

At classical singularities the situation is potentially different. If curvature diverges and density tends to infinity, we have no mechanism guaranteeing a non-uniform distribution over the microstates. In that case, the normalization problem would appear if we tried to treat the singularity's microstates as a uniform discrete set. But that is an argument for showing that the singularity cannot be described by a standard probabilistic theory, not a demonstration that its entropy is zero or infinite. Current physics has no answer: it is an open question that quantum gravity would have to resolve.

Proposal: entropy and probability as relational properties

The preceding subsections show that entropy at a black hole's horizon is well defined and enormous, while at the singularity it is undefined within the classical framework. Does this suggest observer dependence? This annex proposes that it does, but it must be made clear that this is a speculative extrapolation, not a derivation.

An observer external to a black hole has access to a well-defined set of accessible microstates: all the states compatible with the black hole's macroscopic properties as observable from outside — that is, mass, charge, and angular momentum (the only three properties a black hole manifests externally). The probability distribution over those microstates is normalizable. The probabilities sum to 1. The entropy is well defined and enormous, exactly the Bekenstein-Hawking value.

An observer attempting to describe physics from inside the singularity would have no well-defined phase space. The number of potential microstates would be indeterminate. No probability distribution could be consistently normalized. The entropy would be undefined — not because it is zero nor because it is infinite, but because the conceptual framework needed to define it has ceased to be applicable.

The annex's proposal is that this difference is not merely an artefact of the observer's ignorance, but reflects a property of the causal structure: the set of accessible microstates depends on the causal domain from which the description is made. In that sense, entropy and probability would be relational properties, not absolute ones. Both observers apply the same definitions of entropy and probability correctly within their own causal domains. Neither is mistaken. What changes is the set of microstates their framework allows them to define.

The status of this claim must be made explicit. It does not follow from established physics. It is a conceptual interpretation coherent with black hole complementarity (section 3.5) and with the hierarchy of singularities (section 4.3), but it is not the only possible interpretation. A rigorous formulation would require a theory of quantum gravity that does not yet exist.

In section 4.3 we defined S_external = 0 for a causally isolated particle. We can now reformulate this within the proposed relational framework: S_external = 0 because the external observer's causal domain includes no internal microstate of the particle. The set of microstates accessible to the external observer is Ω_external = 1 (a single state: “causally absent particle”), and therefore S_external = k_B ln(1) = 0. Under the relational hypothesis, this is not a property of the particle but a property of the relationship between the particle and the observer, given the causal structure separating them. This reformulation is consistent with the proposal, but does not demonstrate it.

4.5 - Final state and the duality of contrary singularities

The preceding sections have identified two extreme states constituting the operational limits of time within the BHC framework: the singularity of infinite compression (the centre of a black hole) and the singularity of infinite expansion (the final state of complete derealization of a temporal island). This section establishes the duality between those two states. The central argument is that, although they are opposites in terms of density, they produce the same operational result: complete causal isolation, termination of the temporal sequence, and thermal radiation from the boundary.

Consider the two extreme states the framework has derived so far.

State A is the singularity of compression: density tending to infinity, diverging curvature, all light cones pointing towards r = 0, no possible trajectory moving away from the singularity. It is the limit of maximum compression. The boundary delimiting it, the event horizon, emits thermal radiation via the Hawking mechanism.

State B is the singularity of expansion: density tending to zero, separation between constituents tending to infinity, all systems causally disconnected from one another, no interaction possible. It is the limit of maximum dispersion. The boundary delimiting it, the de Sitter cosmological horizon, emits thermal radiation via the Gibbons-Hawking mechanism.

The differences between the two states are evident: in state A the density is infinite, in state B it is zero; in state A the curvature diverges, in state B it is constant and finite; in state A the isolation is by compression, in state B by expansion. But the operational result is identical in both cases: complete causal isolation, time operationally absent for the interior (or exterior, respectively) constituents, and thermal radiation emitted by the causal boundary.

What makes the duality non-trivial is that both states produce that identical result via the same underlying mathematical mechanism: the radiation of causal horizons derived by Gibbons and Hawking. The mechanism does not distinguish between the two types of horizon. Both radiate because both are causal boundaries, regardless of which physical process generated them. In this sense, the singularity of compression and the singularity of expansion are not merely analogous. They are the same phenomenon (causal isolation producing thermal radiation) expressed in the two possible directions of a single variable: density.

It should be noted that the annex uses “singularity” for state B in the operational sense defined in section 4.3.5 (Level 3 of the hierarchy), not in the technical sense of general relativity. De Sitter space is geodesically complete and its geometry is smooth. What is “singular” is the operational description of time: a state where no interaction is possible and where the temporal sequence has lost all content.

The final state as a boundary condition for the return

The key derivative step is the following. If the final state of the temporal island (complete derealization, S_external = 0, total causal isolation) is operationally equivalent to the state of matter inside a black hole from the parent's perspective, then the return of that matter to the parent via Hawking radiation is not an additional mechanism that must be proposed. It is the direct consequence of the duality.

Matter that has reached state B is in a state satisfying exactly the boundary conditions for Hawking emission towards the parent universe. No additional mechanism of “return” is needed: the return is what Hawking radiation is — the thermal emission of a causal boundary separating an interior domain from an exterior one.

From the parent universe's perspective, the black hole emits Hawking radiation slowly over time. From the child's perspective, that radiation is the exterior manifestation of the final state of complete derealization. The two descriptions are causally inaccessible to each other, consistent with the complementarity principle presented in section 3.5, but both are valid within their own causal domains.

The geometric connection

Section 4.9 develops a topological analogy giving geometric support to this duality: a sheet of paper on which pressure is applied from above experiences compression from the upper perspective and expansion from the lower one. The same physical process, the same energy, produces opposite effects depending on which side of the rupture the observer is on. Under this reading, the singularity of compression and the singularity of expansion would not be two separate physical processes coincidentally producing similar effects, but the same process seen from opposite sides of a rupture of the manifold. The child would experience the parent's compression as its own expansion.

This geometric connection is a conceptual tool, not a formal demonstration. Its formalization requires a theory of quantum gravity describing the behaviour of geodesics across a rupture of the manifold. It is presented here to make the duality intuitive, not to ground it. The grounding rests on the Gibbons-Hawking mechanism, which is established physics.

The quantitative expression of the duality

The duality between the two states is not only qualitative: it has a precise quantitative expression developed in the second annex. The two horizons share the same entropic law, entropy proportional to area over four, but exhibit opposite energy-radius relations. For the black hole, increasing the mass increases the horizon radius, $r_{s}\propto M$; for the de Sitter horizon, increasing the cosmological constant decreases it, $r_{dS}\propto \Lambda ^{-1/2}$. The same area thermodynamics therefore operates on inverted energetic relations, and that inversion is the essence of the duality.

This opposition manifests exactly when the relation between the child's cosmological constant and the parent black hole's mass is derived. As shown in the second annex, there are two ways of relating the two horizons across a single boundary: equating their temperatures or equating their entropies. The thermal route imposes that the de Sitter horizon radius be twice the Schwarzschild radius, $r_{dS}=2r_{s}$, while the entropic route imposes that they be equal, $r_{dS}=r_{s}$. Since the cosmological constant scales as $\Lambda \propto 1/r_{dS}^{2}$, that factor-of-two difference in radius translates into an exact factor of four in the value of Λ:

$$\frac{\Lambda _{entròpica}}{\Lambda _{tèrmica}}=4$$

That factor of four is the quantitative signature of the duality of contrary singularities. It is the square of the factor of two separating the surface gravities of the two horizons, which in turn reflects that the same entropic area law operates on energy-radius relations of opposite sign. The duality, established in this section as an operational identity between two extreme states, thus acquires a concrete numerical manifestation: the two horizons not only produce the same operational result via the same mechanism, but their energetic opposition leaves an exact quantitative imprint on the relation connecting the child with the parent.

Epistemological status and limitations

The duality rests on an established basis (the Gibbons-Hawking mechanism applies to any causal horizon) and on assumptions of the annex (that the operational equivalence between the two types of causal isolation is a physical identity and not merely an analogy). A formal demonstration that the two extreme states produce exactly the same result via the same mechanism, and not merely a similar result, requires a theory treating both the extreme-curvature and extreme-expansion regimes simultaneously within the same mathematical framework. That theory does not exist.

It is also worth specifying what the duality does not resolve. It does not resolve the Hawking information problem; it only explains why the return to the parent is possible in principle, without specifying the entropic state in which it occurs (a question developed in section 4.8). Nor does it specify the temporal relationship between the child's evolution and the parent's evaporation, which is what the temporal parenthesis model of section 4.10 addresses explicitly.

4.6 - Emergent w_eff and terminal de Sitter

This section derives that any finite temporal island under ΛCDM experiences, from its internal perspective, an effect equivalent to the Big Rip — the complete dissolution of all structure. This happens without the global cosmological constant changing and without w departing from -1. Terminal de Sitter is not a consequence of phantom energy or exotic physics. It is the inevitable consequence of a feedback mechanism operating within any finite system in the presence of a constant cosmological constant.

The mechanisms of mass loss

A temporal island loses mass/energy through several mechanisms operating simultaneously.

The first is the irreversible conversion of matter into radiation. All the baryonic matter of a temporal island inevitably ends up converted into radiation. There are two possible routes. If the proton's half-life is finite (as extensions of the Standard Model predict, though this has not been experimentally verified), baryonic matter decays directly into lower-energy products. If the proton is stable, the path is longer but the destination is the same: nuclear fusion inside stars converts light elements into increasingly heavy ones up to iron-56, which occupies the minimum of nuclear binding energy. The remaining matter cools and collapses gravitationally to form black holes, which in turn evaporate via Hawking radiation. In either scenario, the final result is the same: all matter is converted into radiation.

The second mechanism is Hawking evaporation of the internal black holes. Black holes within the island emit thermal radiation at temperature T = ħc³/8πGMk_B. The radiated power scales as P ∝ M⁻². In the island's late stages, when r_c has contracted significantly, a growing fraction of that radiation escapes beyond r_c.

The third is the derealization of radiation and relativistic particles. Photons and particles with insufficient energy to remain gravitationally associated with the island progressively move to distances exceeding r_c and, eventually, r_dS. Once derealized, they no longer contribute to M(t).

The combination of these mechanisms produces dM/dt < 0 for all t: the mass of the temporal island is a strictly decreasing function of time.

The contraction of r_c and the feedback mechanism

Since r_c = (3GM/Λc²)^(1/3), and M(t) decreases monotonically, r_c(t) decreases monotonically. As r_c contracts, constituents that were inside the island progressively find themselves outside the new r_c. They are derealized. But their derealization further reduces M(t), which contracts r_c still more. This is positive feedback: M decreases, r_c contracts, new constituents are derealized, M decreases further, r_c contracts further.

The process is self-accelerating and irreversible. Each contraction of r_c produces new derealization, which produces new contraction. The acceleration comes not from any external force but from the structure of the mechanism itself. There is no way to increase M(t) to compensate for the contraction of r_c once the process has begun: the feedback can only advance in one direction.

The emergent effective equation of state

The global equation-of-state parameter w of ΛCDM remains exactly -1. Λ is constant by definition. Nothing has changed on the global cosmological scale.

But from the temporal island's internal perspective, what is relevant is not the global w but the relationship between the energy of the cosmological constant and the gravitational energy of the system. The gravitational binding energy scales as E_grav ∝ -M^(5/3), which decreases with M. As M → 0, E_grav → 0, while the contribution of Λ remains constant. From the internal perspective, the cosmological constant becomes increasingly dominant relative to the system's gravitational energy. This is operationally equivalent to a w_eff moving away from -1 in the direction of phantom energy.

It is necessary to be precise about what is being claimed. The standard Big Rip requires w < -1 globally — that the dark energy density grows with expansion. Here there is no global growth. What there is, is a decrease of the gravitational counterpart, which makes the same constant Λ proportionally more dominant. w_eff is not a modification of standard cosmology: it is an emergent quantity describing the internal behaviour of a finite system, not a global property of spacetime.

The final destiny

The feedback process takes the temporal island through a sequence of stages. In the first, nuclear fusion exhausts the stars' fuel and matter accumulates in white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes. In the second, the remaining baryonic matter converts into radiation (whether by proton decay or by the iron/collapse/evaporation route). In the third, Hawking evaporation exhausts the black holes and the feedback accelerates as r_c contracts. In the fourth, the feedback mechanism makes the rate of mass loss grow in an accelerated way, and w_eff moves progressively away from -1. Finally, r_c → 0: all matter has been derealized. The temporal island has reached state B of the duality of contrary singularities described in section 4.5, with effectively zero density, complete causal isolation, and S_external = 0. Terminal de Sitter has been completed.

The distinction from the standard Big Rip

The standard Big Rip requires w < -1 globally, a finite-time singularity where the scale factor diverges, and the literal tearing apart of all structure, including nuclear and atomic structure. The terminal de Sitter proposed in this annex differs in three respects. First, global w remains -1: there is no modification of standard cosmology. Second, the process occurs asymptotically, not in finite time. Third, the tearing is not mechanical but causal: it is not that forces overcome all structure, but that all structure progressively loses its causal connectivity.

But the final state is operationally indistinguishable: all matter has lost all causal relevance, no structure remains, and the system has reached complete causal isolation. The difference is in the path, not in the destination.

Epistemological status

The feedback mechanism starts from established premises: the criterion of gravitational binding, Hawking evaporation, the constancy of Λ under ΛCDM, and the inevitable conversion of matter into radiation. What the annex adds is the interpretation that w_eff is a physically meaningful quantity from the island's internal perspective, and that the feedback process constitutes a terminal de Sitter. This interpretation is coherent with established physics, but goes beyond what the standard framework usually asserts.

The quantitative rate of the feedback depends on the mass distribution within the island at each moment, which is an extremely complex N-body problem. This annex establishes the qualitative mechanism, but does not calculate its precise rate.

4.7 - Cosmological network of black holes

The previous sections have established a set of proposals: that black holes generate independent temporal domains (4.1), that temporal islands inevitably evolve towards complete derealization (4.6), and that the final state of derealization is operationally equivalent to the interior of a black hole from the parent's perspective (4.5). If we accept these proposals as a working framework, their combination with the established physics of ΛCDM produces a logical consequence: every black hole that forms in a universe generates a child universe that evolves, reaches its final state, and returns its mass-energy to the parent as Hawking radiation. The result is a network of universes connected by black holes.

It is necessary to be explicit about the status of this network. It is not a direct derivation from ΛCDM. Standard cosmology predicts the formation of black holes, but it does not predict that they generate child universes. The network is a logical consequence of the annex's hypotheses (temporal orthogonality, derealization, duality of singularities, rupture mechanism) when applied to ΛCDM. It is not an independent postulate added to the framework, but neither is it derived from established physics alone without the annex's prior hypotheses.

Structure of the network

The fundamental loop is as follows. A parent universe contains matter that, under the thermodynamic evolution described in section 2.3, forms black holes. Each black hole generates a child universe via the rupture mechanism described in section 4.9. The child universe evolves autonomously: it forms its own temporal islands, its own black holes, its own network of descendant generations. Eventually, via the feedback mechanism of section 4.6, the child universe reaches its final state of complete derealization. In this state (state B of the duality of section 4.5), its mass-energy returns to the parent as Hawking radiation. The parent perceives this return as the gradual evaporation of the black hole.

The loop is closed and energetically consistent within the framework of this thesis. The energy the parent loses in forming the black hole is the energy it recovers when the hole evaporates. The Hawking radiation it receives, however, contains the accumulated entropy of the child universe's entire lifetime, not simply the entropy of the matter that originally fell in. The implications of this entropic difference are developed in section 4.8.

The network is not flat but hierarchical. Each child universe generates its own black holes, which generate their own child universes. The depth of the hierarchy is in principle unlimited. A massive universe with a long lifetime (determined by t ∝ M³) can generate a very deep network of descendant generations. A low-mass universe with a short lifetime will generate few descendants.

The distinction from Smolin and other proposals

Smolin's cosmological natural selection (section 3.3) arrives at a similar structure, a network of universes connected by black holes, but by a different route and with additional ingredients of its own: mutation of the physical constants in each generation and natural selection as an optimization mechanism. Poplawski (section 3.6) introduces Einstein-Cartan torsion as a mechanism for avoiding the singularity and generating the child's expansion.

This proposal also introduces ingredients that are not in ΛCDM: temporal orthogonality (4.1), derealization (4.3), the duality of contrary singularities (4.5), and the dimensional rupture mechanism (4.9). The difference from Smolin and Poplawski is that their additional ingredients are of a different nature. Smolin modifies the physical constants between generations. Poplawski modifies the underlying theory of gravity. The annex does neither: its additional proposals are interpretive and conceptual, not modifications of the field equations or of the fundamental constants. This distinction is real, but it does not imply that the annex is less speculative — rather, that it is speculative in a different way.

Initial conditions and properties of the child universes

If each child universe receives its energy via an impulse at the moment of collapse (as section 4.9 proposes), then the child's properties must be determined by the properties of the parent black hole at the moment of formation. The mass of the parent black hole determines the child's total energy. The angular momentum of the parent black hole, described by the Kerr metric, is imprinted on the child's initial geometry via the conservation of angular momentum through the bounce.

These relationships mean the network is not simply a set of independent universes but a structure where the properties of each generation are connected to those of the previous one. The proposed hypotheses are compatible with both models of this transmission.

The first is determinism by initial conditions: the child's properties are completely determined by the mass, spin, and charge of the parent black hole at the moment of collapse. There is no random variation or mutation. The diversity of the network arises from the diversity of parent black hole masses, not from any mutation mechanism.

The second is compatible with Smolin's natural selection: if some stochastic quantum process operates at the rupture and introduces variability in the physical constants, the network would be subject to natural selection. This annex's proposals do not require this mechanism, but neither do they exclude it.

The distinction between the two models is empirically impossible to verify from within any universe of the network, since it would require observational access to multiple generations of universes.

The first model does, however, have a concrete observational consequence. If the angular momentum of the parent black hole is imprinted on the child's initial geometry, the distribution of galactic rotation in the child universe should reflect the parent black hole's spin. This connects directly with the observational signature of galactic rotation asymmetry discussed in section 4.11.

Limitations and open problems

The exact mechanism by which the parent's properties are transmitted to the child requires a theory of quantum gravity describing the behaviour of geodesics through the rupture of the manifold. The depth of the hierarchy (how many generations the network has had since some hypothetical origin) is a question the annex cannot answer. If the network is infinite in depth, the question "which was the first universe?" has no answer within the framework.

4.8 - Entropic accounting between universes

This section addresses a question the BHC framework generates naturally: when the mass-energy of a child universe returns to the parent as Hawking radiation, what is the entropic accounting of that return? The argument has two parts. The first proposes that entropy is not a property of the particles but of the phase space of the manifold in which they exist, and that the child therefore begins with its own entropic counter, independent of the parent. The second acknowledges that the exact nature of the return depends on the resolution of the Hawking information problem, which the proposed model deliberately inherits unresolved.

Entropy as a relational property

Entropy S = k_B ln(Ω) measures the number of accessible microstates compatible with the observed macroscopic state. The central point, often overlooked, is that the accessible microstates are neither an intrinsic property of the particles nor an absolute value of the system: they depend on the frame of description from which they are counted. The phase space that defines which microstates are distinguishable is a construction relative to the observer and to the manifold from which they describe the system. Changing the frame of description changes which microstates are accessible, and therefore changes the entropy, without the underlying information having been modified.

It is worth being very precise about what this reading claims and what it does not, because a careless formulation would lead to a contradiction with the conservation of information, which is a premise of the framework. It is not proposed that entropy is destroyed at the rupture, nor that the child begins with objectively zero entropy in any absolute sense. What is proposed is that entropy is relative to the observer, while total information is always conserved. Information is the invariant; entropy is the part of the description that depends on the causal frame from which it is counted.

The distinction can be made intuitive with an analogy, with the caveat that every analogy has its limits. Imagine a gas released in a corner of a room, and an observer whose eyes are closed during the first moments. When they open their eyes, they take the state they see as their initial state, with a given entropy, and from there they measure how the gas spreads and the entropy grows. That initial state is not, however, the absolute minimum of the system: before they opened their eyes, the gas was more concentrated, with an even lower entropy. The observer does not deny that the earlier state existed; it simply is not the starting point of their counter, because their frame of description begins when they open their eyes.

An observer's initial state is the minimum of their frame, not the absolute minimum of the system.

Applied to the rupture: the child, from its own foliation, has an initial state of minimum entropy, its Big Bang, from which entropy grows consistently with the second law inside the child. That this is the child's minimum does not deny that from the parent's frame there is a description in which the total entropy, including everything that will eventually return, is conserved. The child does not have access to this description of the parent, just as the observer did not see the concentrated gas, but it does not deny it. There is, then, no contradiction between saying that the child begins with minimum entropy and saying that total information and entropy are conserved: the first statement is relative to the child's foliation, the second is the global invariant.

There is, however, an important difference between the gas analogy and the case of the child, which must be made explicit so that the relational reading is not confused with mere subjective entropy. In the gas case, the observer is ignorant of a state that exists within their own temporal frame: had they looked, they would have seen it, and their entropy is subjective through removable ignorance. In the case of the child, the parent's state is inaccessible not through ignorance but through causal structure: the child cannot look back and see the parent, because it belongs to a foliation causally separated by the rupture. The inaccessibility is structural, not epistemic. This is why the relational entropy of the frame is more robust than the subjective entropy of Gibbs or Jaynes: it does not depend on what the observer knows, but on what their causal frame allows them to define.

This reading can be verified with a consistency argument that the Hawking mechanism itself provides. If entropy were objectively destroyed at the rupture, so that the child began with zero entropy in the absolute sense and the information about what entered were lost, then the mass-energy returning to the parent as Hawking radiation would have to emerge stripped of the original information. But Hawking radiation, under the conservation of information and the Page curve, does not behave that way: the entropy of the radiation grows to a maximum around the Page time and then decreases to zero as evaporation completes, guaranteeing that all the information eventually emerges. That the information is recovered in the end confirms that it was never destroyed at the rupture: it was conserved as an invariant, while what differed between parent and child was solely the entropy accessible from each frame. The conservation of information and the relational reading of entropy are therefore two faces of the same structure.

The consequence for the apparent entropic paradox is direct. The question of how high-entropy matter, such as that of a black hole, can produce a low-entropy child universe dissolves once it is understood that entropy is relative to the frame. High entropy is a property of the description from the parent; the child's low initial entropy is a property of the description from its own foliation. It is not that entropy decreases, which would violate the second law, but that the child begins its counter from the minimum of its own frame, while total information is conserved and, in time, returns to the parent.

Synthesis: relational properties of the frame

Sections 4.4.3 and 4.8.1 converge on a common principle: both entropy and probability, at the extreme limits the annex explores, could be understood as relational properties of the frame in which matter exists, not as absolute properties of matter itself.

The observer external to the black hole has access to a well-defined phase space: the macroscopic properties of the black hole observable from outside. Their probability measure is normalizable. Their entropic accounting is continuous from the parent's beginning. The observer internal to the child is born in a new manifold with its own phase space. Their entropic accounting begins from the minimum of their own frame, not from an absolute zero, and information is conserved as a global invariant. The difference between the observers is not one of available information, which is the same and is conserved, but of which causal frame allows them to define the accessible microstates and therefore the entropy. This is the precise relational formulation: information is the shared invariant, and entropy is the quantity relative to the foliation.

Neither of the two observers is incorrect. Each applies the same definitions of entropy and probability correctly within their own causal domain and within their own manifold. The difference is not one of available information but of which frame allows them to define the accessible microstates.

It must be remembered, however, that this synthesis rests on the hypothesis that the rupture of the manifold generates a genuinely new phase space. Without this hypothesis, the argument does not work. Its validation requires a theory of quantum gravity that does not yet exist.

The Hawking information problem as an inherited problem

When the child universe reaches its final state and its mass-energy returns to the parent as Hawking radiation, the parent receives the child's accumulated entropy in some sense. But the exact nature of this return depends on the resolution of the Hawking information problem, which the annex inherits without resolving and without claiming to resolve.

There are three possibilities.

The first is the preservation of information. Hawking radiation carries quantum correlations that encode the information about the child's contents. The parent receives its own entropy and the child's, encoded in the correlation structure of the radiation, consistent with the preservation of quantum unitarity and with the Page curve. Under this possibility, the network is not simply an energy circuit but a circuit of structured information: the parent receives, in principle, a complete record of everything that occurred in the child universe, although in practice that record would be inaccessibly complex.

The second is thermalization. Hawking radiation is purely thermal and carries no structured correlations. The parent receives energy without specific information about the child's internal state. Quantum unitarity is violated. Under this possibility, the network transmits energy but not structure: each generation begins anew with no memory of the previous ones.

The third is the "reset" through rupture of the manifold. If the rupture generates a genuinely new phase space (consistent with the proposal of 4.8), then the child's entropy is a property of the child's manifold that is not transferred directly to the parent. The parent would receive energy but not the child's structured entropy. This possibility is the most speculative and depends entirely on the nature of the rupture, which requires quantum gravity to be formalized.

The framework is compatible with all three possibilities and takes no position on which is correct. Attempting to resolve the Hawking information problem would go beyond what the framework can justify with current tools. The entropic accounting works in its general outlines regardless of which of the three possibilities is correct: the network is energetically closed, energy is conserved in the loop, and the child begins with effectively zero entropy from its internal perspective. The precise nature of the return is an open question that a complete theory of quantum gravity would have to resolve.

That the annex is compatible with all three possibilities is both a virtue and a limitation. It is a virtue because a framework compatible with multiple resolutions of an open problem is more honest than one that arbitrarily assumes a specific resolution. It is a limitation because a framework that cannot discriminate between opposing possibilities at a crucial point will never be able to claim completeness.

Although the framework recognizes the three possibilities as open, it adopts the first, the preservation of information, as a working hypothesis when it needs to describe the rate and structure of the return. The reason for this choice is not arbitrary: the preservation of information is the only one of the three possibilities compatible with the premise of conservation of information the framework has already adopted as a working principle, and on which the entropic derivation of the second annex rests. The other two possibilities, thermalization and complete reset, either violate quantum unitarity or leave the entropic return undefined, and therefore do not allow the mechanism of the return to be described positively.

Under the adopted possibility, the rate at which the child's information returns to the parent is obtained from the Page curve: the information, complete at the boundary from the instant the temporal parenthesis opens, is released gradually at the rate the horizon's thermodynamics imposes, with the entropy of the radiation growing up to the Page time and decreasing thereafter until all the information has emerged. The development of this mechanism, and its connection with the thermodynamic limit of Hawking radiation, is detailed in the temporal parenthesis model of section 4.10. It should be stressed that adopting preservation as a working hypothesis is not equivalent to claiming that the other two possibilities are refuted: the framework remains compatible with all three, but uses the first to describe the return because it is the one coherent with its own principles.

4.9 - The genesis mechanism

The previous sections have established that the final state of a temporal island is operationally equivalent to the interior of a black hole (4.5), that the return to the parent via Hawking radiation closes the energy loop (4.7), and that the entropic accounting of the return is consistent with the framework (4.8). But none of these sections has addressed the fundamental question: what is the physical mechanism connecting the parent's gravitational collapse with the child's expansion? This section provides the mechanism in four parts.

The quantum bounce as the origin of expansion

In classical general relativity, gravitational collapse leads inevitably to the curvature singularity: a point where the equations cease to produce physically meaningful results. As established in sections 2.1 and 4.3, the singularity is not an answer but an indicator of the theory's limit.

The proposal set out in the annex is that, when collapse reaches the density regime where the quantum effects of gravity cannot be ignored (the Planck scale), matter is not compressed to zero volume but undergoes a bounce. The gravitational energy accumulated during collapse does not disappear at the singularity: it is released again as expansion. The child's Big Bang is not a mysterious initial condition but the direct consequence of energy conservation through the bounce.

This proposal aligns qualitatively with the bounce mechanism of loop quantum gravity (Loop Quantum Cosmology), where a quantum repulsive pressure emerges at Planck densities and prevents the singularity, producing a subsequent expansion. This framework does not adopt the formalism of loop quantum gravity, but the qualitative mechanism is analogous. It is presented as a physically motivated conjecture, not as an established derivation.

An important clarification: continuous bounce versus rupture. It is worth distinguishing this framework from continuous-bounce frameworks such as loop quantum gravity or Gaztañaga's model. In those, the manifold remains continuous through the bounce: matter is compressed to a finite minimum and re-expands without the geometry breaking. In the BHC framework, by contrast, the bounce is accompanied by a genuine rupture of the manifold, as developed in the following subsections. The analogy with loop quantum gravity is therefore limited to the fact that both avoid the singularity through quantum effects at the Planck scale; the structural difference is that the BHC framework does not maintain geometric continuity. This distinction is essential for everything that follows, because it is the rupture, and not simply the bounce, that gives meaning to temporal orthogonality and to the child's causal disconnection.

A notable property of the bounce is that it naturally produces a uniform expansion. The matter falling into the black hole is drawn towards the centre by gravity, and the compression is isotropic if the black hole is spherical or nearly spherical. When the bounce occurs, the energy is released in all directions simultaneously from an initially homogeneous state. The result is a uniform and isotropic expansion, exactly what we observe in our universe on large scales and what the cosmological principle presupposes.

The stalemate of infinities and the ontological phase transition

The quantum bounce explains how the energy of collapse becomes expansion. But it does not explain why the child is causally disconnected from the parent, why its expansion occurs in a new independent manifold and not simply in the interior of the parent's black hole. This is the question the stalemate of infinities addresses.

At the moment of the classical singularity, two tendencies operate simultaneously and both tend to infinity. The first is gravitational compression: the curvature of spacetime tends to infinity, all light cones point towards r = 0, and the parent's geometry dominates absolutely. The second is causal disconnection: as the compression intensifies, time dilation at the horizon makes interior time and exterior time diverge, and the event horizon becomes an absolute causal barrier.

These two tendencies cannot be resolved simultaneously within the same manifold. Infinite gravity would require a point of absolute convergence within the parent's manifold. Infinite causal disconnection would require an absolute separation from the parent's manifold. This annex proposes that the resolution of this tension is not that one tendency wins over the other, but that the frame containing both (the parent's manifold) cannot sustain the tension and undergoes a phase transition: the manifold breaks and a new manifold is generated. The parent's infinite compression becomes the origin of the child's expansion because the rupture point is precisely where the two divergent tendencies neutralize one another.

It is necessary to be explicit about the status of this argument. The stalemate of infinities is a conceptual proposal of this annex's speculative framework, not a derivation within the formalism of general relativity. That two quantities diverge simultaneously does not necessarily imply a rupture of the manifold: standard general relativity simply notes that the theory ceases to be valid at the singularity, without pronouncing on what replaces it. The annex proposes a concrete resolution (the rupture), but its formalization requires quantum gravity.

Dimensional genesis

To make the rupture of the manifold intuitive, the annex uses a topological analogy that carries precise conceptual content.

A sheet of paper is a two-dimensional manifold. If we exert pressure on the paper until it tears, the original manifold divides into two separate manifolds. The rupture point is not a point of the original manifold: it is the point where the manifold ceases to exist as a continuous object. The central observation is the following: from above, the applied force points downwards; from below, the same force appears to point upwards. There is no new force and no additional mechanism. The reversal of direction is a consequence of which side of the rupture the observer occupies.

Transposed to the BHC framework: the parent manifold is the manifold, and gravitational compression is the force directed towards r = 0. When the compression exceeds the manifold's limit and produces the rupture, the child finds itself on the opposite side. From the child's side, the same energy appears as expansion, because the rupture has inverted the effective direction of the force relative to the new frame. This observation connects directly with section 4.5: the singularity of compression and the singularity of expansion are the same process seen from opposite sides of the rupture.

The topological foundation: Geroch's theorem

The sheet-of-paper analogy is a pedagogical image, but the rupture the framework proposes has a more rigorous foundation than a simple geometric metaphor. The separation of a manifold into two causally independent regions is not an arbitrary operation: it is a change in the topology of space, and general relativity establishes precise conditions on when and how this change can occur.

The relevant result is Geroch's theorem, proved in 1967. In its original form, it establishes that if we have a compact, time-orientable spacetime with a Lorentzian metric that is nowhere singular, and its boundary consists of two three-dimensional spatial sections, then these two sections must have the same topology, and the spacetime necessarily has the structure of a product of the spatial section with a temporal interval. Put another way, in classical and smooth general relativity, the topology of space cannot change: the space a spacetime begins with is, topologically, the same one it ends with.

The consequence, read in reverse, is what interests the framework. If the topology of space must change — that is, if we want to pass from a connected universe to two separate domains — Geroch's theorem requires that at least one of its hypotheses be violated. The possibilities are exactly three, and the literature classifies them cleanly: either the spacetime contains a singularity, or it contains closed timelike curves, or it loses time-orientability. There is no fourth route within classical general relativity. Any change in the topology of space must pass through one of these three doors.

The BHC framework adopts the first, the singularity, and it does so for reasons the other two make evident. Closed timelike curves would imply that certain timelike trajectories close on themselves, allowing an event to lie in its own past, a violation of causality far more severe and problematic than the mere impossibility of prediction associated with a singularity. The loss of time-orientability would imply that the distinction between past and future ceases to be globally consistent. Both are causal pathologies the framework does not wish to introduce. The singularity, by contrast, is precisely the phenomenon gravitational collapse already produces generically, and it is, as established in sections 2.1 and 4.3, the point where general relativity declares itself incomplete.

The singularity is not consistent with a description of spacetime as a continuous manifold, and it is precisely this inconsistency that opens the possibility of topological change: where the manifold ceases to be a manifold, the topology can change.

Thus the rupture of the manifold the framework proposes is not a postulate added without foundation, but the application of an established theorem. The child's separation from the parent is a change of topology; Geroch's theorem establishes that this change requires a singularity (once closed timelike curves and non-orientability are ruled out for causal reasons); and the singularity of gravitational collapse is precisely where it occurs. Under this reading, the singularity ceases to be merely the end of collapsing matter: it is the topologically necessary point where separation into two causally independent domains becomes possible.

This connection has additional support in recent work on regular black holes — that is, black holes with a de Sitter core instead of a curvature singularity. It has been shown that the simultaneous presence of a de Sitter core and an asymptotically flat exterior region, joined within a single manifold, forces the appearance of closed timelike curves, and that avoiding them requires going beyond classical general relativity and admitting a change of topology. This configuration, a de Sitter core inside a black hole, is essentially that of the BHC framework, and the result confirms that the coexistence of the two regions within a single continuous manifold is problematic: topological separation is not merely an option of the framework but a necessity for avoiding more serious causal pathologies.

As always, precision about the status of this argument must be maintained. Geroch's theorem establishes that a change of topology requires a singularity in classical general relativity. But the singularity, by definition, is where that theory ceases to be valid. A complete description of what happens exactly at the transition point — that is, how the singularity becomes a regular physical separation rather than a catastrophic ending — requires a theory of quantum gravity that does not yet exist. Some current approaches explore precisely smooth topology changes at the Planck scale, without a singularity, but none is developed enough to provide the calculation. What Geroch's theorem contributes to the framework is therefore not the complete formalization of the rupture, but something more modest and nonetheless valuable: the guarantee that the topological separation the framework proposes is not arbitrary, but the only route compatible with causality within general relativity, anchored precisely in the phenomenon — the singularity — that collapse already produces.

Geroch's theorem establishes the possibility of topological change, but it refers to the topology of the spatial sections. A natural objection is how this extends to the complete separation of the manifold, including the temporal axis. The commutation of Schwarzschild coordinates already suggests that time and space exchange roles inside the horizon, but by what mechanism does the manifold separate completely? The answer expressed in this annex is an argument from symmetry. If the manifold breaks at the rupture, the rupture affects the complete manifold, not selectively some coordinates and not others. A manifold is a unitary mathematical object: it cannot break partially in the temporal coordinate while keeping the spatial coordinates continuous without a principled reason justifying the asymmetry. A partial rupture would require an asymmetric treatment of the dimensions that cannot be derived from any established principle. A complete rupture requires no asymmetric treatment: it is the direct consequence of the manifold being a unitary object.

Geroch's theorem therefore grounds the spatial part of the rupture, the separation of the topology of space. The additional separation of the temporal axis, which constitutes the orthogonality developed in section 4.1, is an extension going beyond what Geroch establishes, and belongs to the new physics of the rupture: the theorem guarantees that topological separation is possible and necessary via the singularity, and the symmetry argument extends this separation to the entire manifold, but the complete formalization of how the temporal axis becomes orthogonal remains part of the physics the framework identifies without developing.

It must be made precise that the rupture does not destroy dimensions: it separates them. The parent continues to exist with its three spatial dimensions and its temporal dimension. The child exists with its own three spatial dimensions and its own temporal dimension, causally independent of the parent's. No dimensions are lost: there are dimensions that cease to be shared. When the paper tears, the two halves do not lose their dimensions. Each half remains a manifold. What has been lost is the connection between the two, not the manifolds themselves.

This clarification is necessary to avoid an intuitive objection: if the child were embedded within the parent's space (if the spatial dimensions were continuous), the child's position in the parent's space would in principle be observable. It would have to produce measurable gravitational effects or other detectable signatures. The fact that we observe no consistent signal of child universes housed within our space is consistent with, though not proof of, the complete rupture.

Single-impulse model

The previous sections have established the qualitative mechanism of genesis. This subsection makes its energetic structure precise.

The single-impulse model establishes that the child universe receives all its energy in a single event: the bounce at the moment of collapse. There is no continuous feeding from the parent. There is no persistent energetic connection between parent and child once the rupture has occurred. The child universe evolves autonomously with the energy it received at the moment of its origin.

The consequences of this model are direct. The child's properties (its total energy, its initial geometry, its mass-energy distribution) are determined by the properties of the parent black hole at the moment of collapse. A black hole of mass M will produce a child with a total energy proportional to M. A rotating black hole (described by the Kerr metric) will imprint its angular momentum on the child's initial geometry via the conservation of angular momentum.

An important consequence is the constancy of Λ within the child. The energy of the kick is finite and was transmitted in a single instant. Λ is not an abstract property of the vacuum but the representation of the residual energy of the initial kinetic kick in the language of the Friedmann equations. It appears constant because the kick was a punctual event that has already completed: nothing can modify it afterwards.

The energy of the kick scales with the mass of the parent black hole, and therefore the child's Λ is determined by that mass. The precise functional form of this dependence is derived in the second annex from the condition of entropic equality between the horizons, and turns out to be

$$\Lambda _{entròpica}=\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}M^{2}}$$

that is, inversely proportional to the square of the parent's mass. This inverse dependence may seem counterintuitive, since one might expect a more energetic kick to produce a larger Λ, but it is precisely what the duality of section 4.5 predicts: a more massive black hole generates a larger horizon, and a larger de Sitter horizon corresponds to a smaller cosmological constant. Each black hole therefore produces a child with its own cosmological constant determined by the initial conditions of the collapse, with the more massive black holes generating larger child universes with lower background curvature, and the less massive ones generating smaller children with larger Λ.

The single-impulse model also resolves a potential objection about accretion. Black holes in the parent continue accreting matter long after their formation. If the child were continuously fed by this accretion, its evolution would be very different from the one the annex describes. The model resolves this objection: matter accreted afterwards does not enter the original child. As developed in section 4.10, each subsequent accretion event results in its own independent temporal parenthesis within the black hole, without interacting with the original child universe. The child received its energy at the moment of the initial collapse and has been evolving autonomously ever since.

The limitations of this mechanism must be acknowledged. A quantitative description of the bounce requires a complete theory of quantum gravity. The annex establishes the qualitative mechanism, but cannot calculate the precise relationship between the energy of collapse and the energy of the bounce from the dynamics of the rupture.

Nevertheless, the functional form of Λ's dependence on mass can be established by an entropic route, as shown in the second annex, even though that derivation does not come from the dynamics of the bounce but from the thermodynamics of horizons. The connection between the kinetic kick and inflation is qualitatively plausible, but not quantitatively derived: the duration of inflation, its temperature, and its primordial fluctuations cannot be calculated within the current framework. The whole of section 4.9 is presented as a physically motivated and internally consistent conjecture, not as an established derivation.

4.10 - The temporal parenthesis model

Statement of the problem

The principle of orthogonality established in section 4.1 finds its concrete function here. This section develops the temporal parenthesis model: the description of how the temporal axes of parent and child are structurally related. Before presenting the model, however, the question that motivates it must be addressed, because it is a question every black hole cosmology framework has to answer and that most, including continuous-bounce models, tend to sidestep: what temporal relationship holds between the parent's axis and the child's axis?

The question is not a secondary technical detail. Without an answer, nothing precise can be said about when the child's Big Bang occurs relative to the parent's time, nor about how information is conserved through the transition, nor about what exactly it means for matter to be derealized. Every claim about the causal or informational connection between parent and child implicitly presupposes some position on their temporal relationship. Making this position explicit, and arguing for it, is the purpose of this section.

The strategy followed here is not to postulate an answer and defend it, but to examine the possible options and arrive at the most defensible by elimination. There are three conceivable ways of relating the two temporal axes: an instant-by-instant correspondence, a relationship mediated by a variable dilation coefficient, and a strict independence in which neither axis projects onto the other. The first two are ruled out for concrete reasons developed below; the third, strict orthogonality, is the one the framework adopts, not because it is the only one logically conceivable, but because it is the only one that introduces no free parameters and contradicts none of the physical scales in play. Once established, it is shown to resolve several problems as well, which makes it a productive choice and not merely a residual one.

The temporal scales in play

The elimination argument rests on quantitative facts about the temporal scales of the two domains. It is worth establishing them precisely before building the reasoning, because it is the magnitude of their differences that makes the first two options untenable.

Consider first the child's lifetime, measured along its own temporal axis, from the Big Bang to the terminal de Sitter state. This duration depends on which physical processes are considered operative. If proton decay exists, with an estimated half-life between 10³¹ and 10³⁶ years, all baryonic matter will have dissolved on this scale, and the practical de Sitter state, with all material structure undone and the internal black holes evaporated, is reached around 10¹⁰⁰ years. If proton decay does not exist, the road to pure de Sitter passes through much slower quantum tunnelling processes, and the scale extends to magnitudes of order 10^(10⁷⁶) years. In either case, the child's lifetime is astronomically long along its own axis.

Consider now the projection the parent observes: the black hole's evaporation time by Hawking radiation. This quantity scales as t_evap ∝ M³, and for the mass the entropic relation of the second annex assigns to our universe's parent black hole, of order 10⁵³ kg, it gives an evaporation time of order 10¹⁴¹ years. This is a finite and well-defined quantity, measured entirely in the parent's time.

Finally, a third scale must be recalled, already present in the standard physics of collapse: the finite proper time of the observer falling through the horizon, contrasted with the formally infinite time the external observer attributes to the same crossing through gravitational dilation. This divergence between proper time and coordinate time is not a peculiarity of the BHC framework, but a result of general relativity already established in section 2.1, which acquires a new relevance here.

These three scales — the child's internal lifetime, the projected evaporation time, and the divergence between proper time and external time — are the facts on which the elimination argument is built. Their common feature is that no pair of them admits a simple reconciliation through any reasonable proportionality factor.

First option ruled out: instant-by-instant correspondence

The simplest hypothesis is that a direct correspondence exists between the parent's instants and the child's: to each moment of the parent's clock there corresponds a determinate moment of the child's clock, so that both axes advance synchronously, perhaps with a constant proportionality factor between their rates. This is the hypothesis any description would implicitly assume if it treated the black hole interior as a simple extension of the parent's spacetime.

This option is untenable for two independent reasons, both derived from the scales of the previous subsection.

The first is the incompatibility of magnitudes. The child's lifetime along its axis (between 10¹⁰⁰ and 10^(10⁷⁶) years) and the black hole's evaporation time in the parent's time (of order 10¹⁴¹ years) maintain no constant proportionality relation that is physically natural. If a 1-1 correspondence with a constant factor were imposed, that factor would have to take an arbitrary value, with no grounding in the physics of the system, chosen solely to make two scales match that have no reason to be proportional. Moreover, the factor's value would change depending on which processes (proton decay or not) are considered operative within the child, which reveals that it is not a physical constant but a fitting artefact.

The second reason is more fundamental and does not even depend on the numerical values. Already in the standard physics of collapse, the finite proper time of infalling matter and the infinite coordinate time the external observer attributes to it show that there is no well-defined instant-by-instant correspondence between the interior history and exterior time, not even within a single continuous manifold. The external observer never sees matter cross the horizon; internal time, by contrast, elapses and completes the crossing in a finite proper duration. If not even within standard general relativity is there a 1-1 correspondence between the two sides of the horizon, still less can there be one in a framework where the interior constitutes a causally independent domain.

The conclusion of this subsection is therefore that no constant conversion factor can reconcile the two temporal axes. Instant-by-instant correspondence is ruled out both by the values of the scales and by the causal structure of the horizon. It remains to examine whether a more flexible relationship, mediated by a coefficient that varies through the process, could save the idea of a direct temporal connection. That is the second option, analysed next.

Second option ruled out: the variable dilation coefficient

With constant correspondence ruled out, a more flexible possibility remains: that the relationship between the two axes is not constant but varies through the process. The intuition supporting this option is attractive and deserves to be taken seriously. We know that, as the black hole evaporates, its mass decreases and the time dilation associated with the horizon intensifies; the Hawking temperature grows as T ∝ 1/M and the rate of evaporation accelerates. One might therefore think that the relationship between the child's time and the parent's time is not fixed but evolves: that as the black hole shrinks, each interval of the parent's time corresponds to an ever larger portion of the child's time, so that the child would run "ever faster" as seen from the parent.

This option, despite its initial plausibility, is ruled out for three reasons.

The first is that it is intrinsically ad hoc. Any function relating the two temporal axes and adjusted so that the scales match is a free parameter — or rather an entire free function — introduced by hand. The BHC framework has been built on the commitment not to add new fields or parameters beyond those general relativity and standard cosmology already contain. Introducing a variable dilation coefficient, with a functional form chosen specifically to reconcile the child's lifetime with the parent's evaporation time, violates that commitment in the most direct way possible: there is no physical principle within the framework that fixes the form of this function, so any choice is arbitrary and non-predictive.

The second reason is geometric. Establishing a conversion coefficient between the two axes, whether constant or variable, amounts to claiming that a well-defined projection of one axis onto the other exists: that to each portion of the child's time one can assign, via the conversion function, a corresponding portion of the parent's time. But this is precisely what the signature commutation inside the horizon does not permit. As will be argued in the following subsection, the coordinate that acquires temporal character inside the horizon is the one the parent perceives as spatial. If the child's temporal axis points in a direction the parent classifies as spatial, there is no natural projection of one axis onto the other, and speaking of a conversion coefficient, even a variable one, assumes a relationship the geometry does not support.

The third reason is that the plausibility of the initial intuition rests on a confusion. The growing time dilation near the horizon describes how the parent perceives the processes occurring at the horizon itself, not how the child's internal time flows relative to the parent's. That Hawking radiation accelerates towards the end of evaporation is a fact about the thermodynamics of the horizon as seen from the parent, and implies nothing about the child's internal rate. Attributing the acceleration of evaporation to an acceleration of the child's life is to mix two things belonging to different reference frames.

The joint conclusion of the two elimination subsections is that every attempt to establish a direct temporal relationship between the two axes, whether by a constant factor or a variable function, either contradicts the physical scales, or introduces ad hoc structure, or presupposes a geometric projection that the signature commutation does not permit. A single coherent option remains, and it is the one the framework adopts.

The adopted option: strict orthogonality

If instant-by-instant correspondence is untenable and the variable coefficient is ad hoc, the most sober position is that no projection of one axis onto the other exists. The child's entire lifetime, from the Big Bang to complete derealization, corresponds, from the parent, to a single indeterminate instant: that of the black hole's formation. The two temporal axes are orthogonal in the strict sense that no interval of one projects onto the other. It is not that the child's time passes very fast or very slowly as seen from the parent; it is that the very category of "seen from the parent, in units of its time" does not apply to the child's internal life.

This is reached, and it is important to stress it, by elimination. Strict orthogonality is not postulated as a first hypothesis but remains as the only option that does not suffer the defects of the other two: it requires no arbitrary factor, contradicts no scales, and presupposes no illegitimate projection. Among the available possibilities, it is the one that introduces the least unjustified structure.

The geometric foundation of this position is the coordinate commutation of the Schwarzschild metric inside the horizon, established in section 2.1. Outside the horizon, the radial coordinate r is spatial and the coordinate t is temporal. Inside the horizon, their roles are exchanged: r acquires temporal character and t acquires spatial character. This is neither a convention nor an artefact of Schwarzschild coordinates, but a property of the metric's signature: the metric term associated with r changes sign on crossing the horizon, and with it the causal character of the coordinate. Inside the horizon, advance towards decreasing r is as inevitable as advance towards the future is outside: it is the obligatory temporal direction.

The interpretation this annex proposes is that this obligatory temporal direction of the interior, pointing towards decreasing values of a coordinate the parent perceives as spatial, is the child's temporal axis. The light cones of the interior orient towards this direction, that is, towards where the parent locates space. A temporal axis pointing where another observer sees space is, in the precise sense the framework gives the term, orthogonal to that observer's temporal axis. Signature commutation is therefore the geometric signature of orthogonality.

It is necessary to be explicit about the epistemological status of this interpretation, as has been done at every original step of the framework. Coordinate commutation is an established datum of general relativity; nobody disputes it. But general relativity interprets it as a phenomenon internal to a continuous manifold, not as the beginning of a genuinely independent temporal axis. Reading it as genuine orthogonality is a proposal of the present work, not a consequence of the standard formalism, and its complete formalization requires the physics of the rupture of section 4.9. Strict orthogonality is therefore an interpretive choice, the most defensible among those available, not a proven theorem. This distinction is essential: what is claimed is not that orthogonality follows necessarily from general relativity, but that it is the most coherent and least assumption-laden reading once the rupture hypothesis has been adopted.

Correspondence with the Penrose diagrams

The Penrose-Carter diagrams, introduced in section 2.1, represent the complete causal structure of a spacetime in a finite region, and offer a visual representation of the preceding argument. In the diagram of a Schwarzschild black hole, the singularity appears as a horizontal line at the top, which confirms its character as a surface in the future and not a point in space. The event horizon appears as a diagonal line at forty-five degrees. The coordinate commutation becomes visible in the fact that lines of constant r, vertical and therefore spatial outside the horizon, become horizontal and therefore temporal inside the horizon.

The standard interpretation of this diagram is that of general relativity: the interior is an extension of the same manifold, with a different causal structure but with no break. The BHC framework's interpretation reads the same diagram differently. The change of orientation of the constant-r lines at the horizon is the geometric representation of the decoupling of the temporal axis. The black hole interior in the diagram is not, under this reading, the interior of a continuous manifold, but the projection of an independent temporal axis onto the plane of the parent's diagram. The singularity at the top, the horizontal line, is not the point where matter arrives and ends, but the intersection of the child's temporal axis, having completed its course, with the plane of the diagram. The singularity is not where matter accumulates: it is where the temporal parenthesis closes.

It is worth being explicit, here too, about the status of this reading. Penrose diagrams are tools of standard general relativity, and the diagram itself is not modified. What is proposed is a different interpretation of the same diagram, not an alteration of its structure. The coordinate commutation visible in the diagram is the shared physical datum; reading it as the signature of the temporal parenthesis is the framework's original contribution.

Why orthogonality is productive: the problems it resolves

So far, strict orthogonality has been presented as the most sober option by elimination. But an interpretive choice gains solidity not only by being the least problematic, but by its fruitfulness: by the number of questions it resolves without need of additional assumptions. This subsection shows that orthogonality, far from being a merely residual position, illuminates several problems that would otherwise lack a natural answer.

The first is the frozen star problem. One of the well-known results of general relativity is that, for an external observer, an object falling towards a black hole's horizon never appears to reach it. Gravitational time dilation makes the falling object's time, seen from outside, slow progressively until it formally halts at the horizon. The external observer never sees the object cross the surface: they see it frozen, redshifted to invisibility, but formally present at the horizon forever. From the perspective of the freely falling observer, by contrast, there is nothing special at the horizon: they cross it in a finite proper time and continue with no notable local experience.

The complementarity of section 3.5 reconciles these two descriptions by establishing that no observer can access both at once. The temporal parenthesis model offers a deeper reading of this reconciliation. The external observer sees matter frozen at the horizon because the interior history does not elapse along the parent's temporal axis, but along the orthogonal axis. From the parent's time, indeed, nothing advances inside the black hole: the freezing is literally correct in the sense that no internal process occurs in the parent's time. But it is not that matter is suspended in a static state; it is that its complete temporal evolution elapses in a direction the parent cannot measure. The freezing from outside and the complete life from inside are not two contradictory descriptions of one reality that must be reconciled, but two descriptions of two independent temporal axes, each correct within its own axis. There is no contradiction because there is no claim about one and the same temporal axis.

The second problem orthogonality resolves is the incompatibility of temporal scales that motivated the entire elimination argument. Under strict orthogonality, the child's lifetime (between 10¹⁰⁰ and 10^(10⁷⁶) years along its axis) and the parent's evaporation time (of order 10¹⁴¹ years) no longer have to be reconciled, because they do not belong to the same axis. The question of which of the two is longer ceases to make sense, just as it makes no sense to ask whether a horizontal distance is longer than a temporal interval. The incompatibility that made the other options untenable disappears not because it is resolved, but because it dissolves: it was a problem generated by the assumption that the two times were comparable.

The third problem, and the most delicate, is how the child's information can be conserved and return to the parent without any direct temporal relationship between the two. Here orthogonality combines with the established thermodynamics of horizons to give a precise answer. The conservation of information, adopted as a premise of the framework, implies that all of the child's information is preserved. Under strict orthogonality, this information is completely present at the boundary from the single instant, in the parent's time, at which the parenthesis opens.

The natural question is therefore why this information does not return to the parent instantaneously, but over the aeons that evaporation lasts.

The answer is that the rate of return is limited not by the availability of the information but by the thermodynamics of the horizon. The boundary behaves as a black body at the Hawking temperature, and the power it can radiate is fixed by the Stefan-Boltzmann law:

$$P=\sigma AT^{4}$$

where A is the horizon area and T the Hawking temperature. Since the area scales as $A\propto M^{2}$ and the temperature as $T\propto 1/M$, the radiated power turns out to be

$$P\propto M^{2}\cdot \frac{1}{M^{4}}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}$$

inversely proportional to the square of the mass. However completely the child's information is already present at the boundary, it cannot be released faster than this thermodynamic ceiling permits. Hawking radiation does not come out all at once because a black body cannot radiate at a rate higher than its temperature and area impose. Gradual release is therefore not a consequence of any limitation in the availability of the information, but a direct consequence of the thermodynamics of the horizon.

This picture has a formal correlate in the current physics of the information problem. The Page curve describes how the entropy of the radiation emitted by a black hole evolves over evaporation: it grows initially, reaches a maximum around half the black hole's lifetime (the Page time) and then decreases to zero as evaporation completes, guaranteeing that all the information eventually emerges. Within the temporal parenthesis framework, this curve describes the rate at which the child's information, complete at the boundary from the instant of opening, projects onto the parent's temporal axis. Early radiation appears thermal because the information is encoded in subtle correlations that only become legible once enough radiation has emerged. The Stefan-Boltzmann ceiling and the Page curve jointly determine both the rate and the structure of the release, without at any point requiring a temporal correspondence between the child's interior and the parent's time.

It is worth stressing that this resolution requires no physics beyond the established thermodynamics of horizons combined with orthogonality. The conservation of information is a premise; the rate of release is a known result; and orthogonality is what allows one to claim that the information is complete at the boundary from the outset without this forcing an instantaneous release. The three pieces fit together with no added assumptions.

Mass as a regulator of complexity

The evaporation time of a black hole in the parent's time scales as t_evap ∝ M³, a direct result of Hawking physics. Under the BHC framework, the mass of the parent black hole plays a double role that deserves precise analysis, because it provides the occasion for an important epistemological distinction.

First, as the single-impulse model of section 4.9 establishes, the child's properties are determined by M at the moment of collapse: its total energy, its initial geometry, the intensity of the kinetic kick that sets it in motion. A low-mass black hole produces a child with little initial energy, scant capacity to form structure, few internal black holes, and a meagre network of descendants. A massive black hole produces a child with much energy, a rich expansion, many internal black holes, and a deep network of descendants. The black hole's mass therefore determines the child's capacity for complexity: the larger M is, the more complex the universe it contains can become.

Second, that same M determines the black hole's evaporation time in the parent's time, via t_evap ∝ M³. These are two independent consequences of the same initial parameter. The mass determines, from the parent, how long the evaporation process lasts; and it determines, from the child, how much structural richness is possible along its own temporal axis. Both dependencies are real, but they operate in different reference frames.

A natural temptation must be avoided, however: claiming that the evaporation time is the measure, in the parent's time, of the duration of the child's life. This reading is attractive because it elegantly connects the two dependencies, but it cannot be justified within the framework, and in fact contradicts it. As established throughout this section, there is no common temporal frame between parent and child allowing intervals of one to be measured in units of the other. The evaporation time is an observation of the parent that depends on M; the duration of the child's life is a quantity of the child that also depends on M. That both depend on the same parameter does not imply that one measures the other, just as two quantities depending on the same variable are not, for that reason, the same quantity.

What can be claimed rigorously is a correlation through common dependence: a more massive black hole simultaneously has two properties, a longer evaporation time and a child universe with more capacity for complexity. These two properties appear together because both are functions of M, not because they are synchronized instant by instant. Mass is a regulator of complexity in the sense that it governs the child's potential richness, but not in the sense that the evaporation time is a direct projection of that richness onto the parent's temporal axis. This distinction, between correlation through common dependence and direct measurement, is precisely the kind of precision the framework must maintain in order not to claim more than its principles allow.

Derealized matter and its return to the parent

The previous subsections have established that the child's information returns to the parent as Hawking radiation, at a rate fixed by the thermodynamics of the horizon. It remains to specify the status of the child's matter throughout this process, because it is here that the temporal parenthesis model connects with the notion of derealization of section 4.3 and with the duality of singularities of section 4.5.

Throughout the child's lifetime, matter is progressively derealized: as accelerated expansion drags regions ever further beyond the child's causal horizon, its constituents lose the possibility of interacting and, in the terminal de Sitter limit, causal isolation becomes complete. In this terminal state, derealized matter no longer participates in any sequence of events: it has lost the relational content that operationally defines time, as established in section 4.3.2. The framework's proposal is that, at this limit, derealized matter ceases to have a spacetime of its own in the operational sense: it belongs to no region where causal relations define an active geometry. It becomes, in this sense, pure information encoded at the boundary, with no spacetime substrate sustaining it as localized matter.

It is this loss of spacetime substrate that makes its return to the parent conceivable. An entity retaining a well-defined spacetime of its own would be anchored to its domain and could not cross the boundary towards the parent. Derealized matter, by contrast, having lost that anchorage, can return to the parent's domain through the mechanism the parent perceives as Hawking radiation. Hawking radiation is, under this reading, the manifestation in the parent's time of the child's matter that has completed its derealization and returns to the parent's manifold. The conservation of information, a premise of the framework, guarantees that what returns preserves the informational content of what entered, though not its organization as structured matter.

The limit of this description must be marked with the same frankness applied to the rest of the framework. The claim that derealized matter loses its own spacetime and returns by tunnelling through the boundary is an interpretive proposal depending on the physics of the rupture of section 4.9, which is not completely formalized. What can be established rigorously is that the picture is internally coherent: the conservation of information, derealization as loss of relational content, and Hawking radiation as the channel of return fit together without contradiction. The complete formalization of how each component of matter crosses the boundary remains an open problem.

Accreted matter and nested parentheses

A natural objection to the temporal parenthesis model is that black holes do not form only by initial collapse but continue accreting matter throughout their lives. If every black hole generates a child universe, what is the effect on the child of matter accreted afterwards?

The answer is that accreted matter does not enter the original child universe. The child received all its energy at the moment of the initial collapse, according to the single-impulse model of section 4.9, and its life has unfolded completely along its own independent temporal axis. When new matter crosses the black hole's horizon in the parent's time, the child has already completed its life along the orthogonal axis; it has reached its terminal de Sitter state and complete causal isolation. Accreted matter does not arrive at an evolving universe, but at a state where there is nothing to interact with.

What accreted matter does is form its own independent temporal parenthesis. Each accretion event, each mass that crosses the horizon, opens a new parenthesis, a new temporal domain that evolves autonomously along its own axis to its own final state. The black hole, in the parent's time, is therefore a structure of nested parentheses: the primary parenthesis is the original child, born in the initial collapse, and each subsequent accretion adds a new nested parenthesis, a new independent temporal island that lives and dies at its own rate. The total mass of the black hole at any instant is the sum of the mass of the original child and that of all the nested parentheses created up to then.

This picture has a consequence for the interpretation of the evaporation time. The black hole's total evaporation time does not simply reflect the original child's lifetime, but the sum of all the parentheses: the original child and all those nested by accretion. Each parenthesis must complete — that is, reach its final state and return its mass to the parent — for the black hole to evaporate completely. The relation t_evap ∝ M³ must therefore be interpreted as the measure of the total complexity of all the contained parentheses, not just of the original child, always with the caution established in the subsection on mass as regulator: it is a correlation through common dependence, not a direct measure of internal durations.

This picture of nested parentheses is coherent with the single-impulse model and does not contradict it. The model applies to each parenthesis individually: the original child receives its energy in a single impulse at the collapse, and each nested parenthesis receives its own in a single impulse at the moment the accreted mass crosses the horizon. There is no continuous feeding of any parenthesis; each receives its kick and evolves autonomously. The difference between the original child and the nested parentheses is one of scale and context, not of mechanism. It is also worth not confusing nested parentheses with the hierarchy of generations of the network described in section 4.7: the hierarchy of generations is the succession of parent-child universes through the network, while nested parentheses are the temporal islands within a single black hole in a single universe. They are two different kinds of structure within the framework.

The closing condition of the parenthesis

The model requires a closing condition: which state of the child allows the parenthesis to close and the mass-energy to return to the parent? The answer is the terminal de Sitter state established in section 4.6. The parenthesis closes when the child reaches a state where time no longer has an operational definition, because, as established in section 4.3.2, time is operationally defined through the interactions between constituents, and when no interactions are possible, when all constituents have been derealized and causal isolation is complete, time loses its operational content.

It is not that time stops in any absolute sense, but that the sequence of events defining it relationally has ceased to exist. In a completely empty de Sitter universe there are no clocks, no interactions, and no processes that could mark the passage of time; time is undefined not through a limitation of measurement but through absence of content. The child does not end with a dramatic event or a curvature singularity, but when the temporal sequence ceases to apply. The parenthesis closes through the exhaustion of its content, not through any external mechanism, and on closing, the child's mass-energy, having reached the terminal state of the duality of section 4.5, returns to the parent as Hawking radiation.

Synthesis and limits

The temporal parenthesis model proposes that the child's temporal axis is orthogonal to the parent's, so that the child's entire life, from the Big Bang to terminal derealization, corresponds to a single indeterminate instant in the parent's time. This position has not been postulated as a first hypothesis but reached by elimination: instant-by-instant correspondence is untenable through the incompatibility of scales and the causal structure of the horizon, and the variable dilation coefficient is ad hoc, geometrically illegitimate, and based on a confusion of reference frames. Strict orthogonality is the only option that introduces no unjustified structure, and the signature commutation of the Schwarzschild metric inside the horizon gives it its geometric foundation.

Once adopted, orthogonality proves fertile: it resolves the frozen star problem as a matter of two independent axes rather than a contradiction to be reconciled, it dissolves the incompatibility of temporal scales that made the other options untenable, and, combined with the established thermodynamics of horizons, it explains how the child's information can be complete at the boundary from the instant of opening and nevertheless return to the parent gradually, at the rate the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the Page curve impose. The black hole's mass appears as a regulator of the child's complexity, with the qualification that this is a correlation through common dependence and not a direct measure of durations. Subsequent accretion generates independent nested parentheses, and the parenthesis closes when the child reaches the terminal de Sitter state where time loses its operational content.

It remains, finally, to mark the limits with the frankness the framework demands. Strict orthogonality is an interpretive choice, the most defensible among those available, not a theorem derived from general relativity. Its complete formalization, like that of the rupture of the manifold of section 4.9, depends on physics the framework identifies but does not develop: how the two axes relate geometrically at the boundary, how each component of matter crosses the rupture, and how the loss of spacetime substrate of derealized matter is formalized are questions that remain open. What the model establishes is not a demonstration of these transitions, but that its orthogonal interpretation is internally coherent, resolves more problems than it creates, and is consistent with the rest of the framework and with established thermodynamics. In this sense, the temporal parenthesis model is one of the work's central interpretive proposals, and at the same time one of those that most clearly signal where a future formalization would be needed.

4.11 - Observational signatures

This section presents the observational signatures of the BHC framework: the points where the predictions expressed in this annex differ from those of standard cosmology and that would, in principle, allow the two frameworks to be distinguished. The signatures presented have very different epistemological statuses: one is a contested observational indication, one is a causal explanation of an established numerical fact, one is the resolution of a classical cosmological problem, and one is a theoretical discriminator not verifiable with current technology. None of them constitutes a confirmation of the framework, but together they establish that the annex is not empirically empty: it makes predictions and offers explanations that differ from those of standard cosmology in ways that are, in principle, observable or conceptually significant.

Galactic rotation asymmetry

If the angular momentum of the parent black hole is imprinted on the child's initial geometry at the moment of the bounce, as the single-impulse model combined with the conservation of angular momentum proposes, the distribution of galactic rotation in the child universe should reflect the parent black hole's spin. Structure formation in the child occurred within a geometry that had a preferred rotational orientation inherited from the parent, and galaxies forming within that geometry will tend to rotate preferentially in the direction consistent with the initial angular momentum.

Under ΛCDM, there is no natural mechanism for producing a global rotation asymmetry. Galaxy formation is a local process determined by statistically isotropic primordial fluctuations, and there is no reason why it should produce a preferred direction of rotation on cosmological scales.

Lior Shamir has analysed galaxy rotation in large observational samples and identified a statistically significant asymmetry: a slightly larger number of galaxies appearing to rotate in one direction than in the other across the observable universe. If this asymmetry is real and not a consequence of systematic biases in the instruments, in the interpretation, or in the classification algorithms, it would imply that the universe has a global rotational preference, what is known as cosmological helicity.

It is necessary to be honest about the status of this result. Shamir's data are contested: several independent analyses have questioned whether the observed asymmetry is physical in nature or reflects systematic biases, and there is no consensus on the reality of the effect. The annex presents this result as an indication consistent with its predictions, not as a confirmation. The distinction matters: the BHC framework predicts a global rotation asymmetry as a direct consequence of the conservation of angular momentum through the bounce. That observational data exist which could be consistent with this prediction is relevant, but not conclusive.

Coincidence of the Hubble radius and the Schwarzschild radius

The Hubble radius, the distance to the boundary of the observable universe defined as c/H₀, is approximately equal to the Schwarzschild radius that would correspond to all the mass-energy contained within that observable radius. If one took all the matter and energy of the observable universe and calculated what black hole they would form, the resulting radius would be of the order of the Hubble radius. This coincidence is a recognized and accepted numerical fact.

In ΛCDM, the coincidence is attributed to the flatness of the universe. A flat universe lies exactly at the critical density ρ_c = 3H₀²/8πG, which is precisely the density at which the Schwarzschild radius of the mass contained within the Hubble radius coincides with the Hubble radius. The coincidence and flatness are mathematically related.

Under the BHC framework, this coincidence acquires a structural origin. If our universe is the interior of a black hole of the parent, its total mass-energy was determined by the mass of the parent black hole at the moment of collapse, and the Schwarzschild radius of that mass is the radius of the parent black hole. The coincidence between the child's Hubble radius and the parent's Schwarzschild radius is, in this sense, a manifestation of the same geometric condition that runs through the whole framework: the identification of the Schwarzschild horizon with the cosmological horizon, r_s = r_dS, which the second annex derives from the entropic equality between the two horizons.

It is necessary to be precise, however, about the status of this explanation, because it is the same one established in the second annex and must not be overstated. The condition r_s = r_dS is a structural consistency of the framework with ΛCDM, not an independent prediction of the numerical value. As demonstrated in the second annex, every global cosmological quantity that could be used to verify the coincidence already contains the cosmological constant through the Friedmann equations, so the framework does not extract the value of the coincidence without prior knowledge of some quantity that already contains it. What the framework contributes is therefore not a prediction of the coincidence's numerical value, but a structural origin for a fact that in ΛCDM appears as a property of flatness with no deeper cause. The framework places the coincidence within a conceptual structure where it ceases to be an isolated accident, but without claiming a quantitative prediction that the Friedmann circularity does not permit.

Flatness and the horizon problem

The question of the universe's flatness deserves particular care, because it is a point where a naive reading of the framework could claim more than its principles permit, and honesty about this limit is essential.

One might think the BHC framework explains flatness as a direct geometric inheritance: if the child is born from a spherically symmetric collapse, it should be born flat. This reading, however, does not hold, and the reason is structural. The framework proposes a genuine rupture of the manifold, and the rupture conserves the mass-energy content but not the geometry — that is, not the initial rate of expansion. Flatness, Ω = 1, is precisely the exact tuning between energy density and expansion rate, ρ = 3H²/8πG. If the rupture conserves the energy but leaves the expansion rate undetermined, the relationship between the two, which is what fixes flatness, is not determined by the rupture.

Flatness is therefore not an automatic inheritance from the symmetry of the collapse: it is a specific and finely tuned value that the rupture, by its very nature, cannot transmit. Genuine rupture and inherited flatness are, in this sense, structurally incompatible, and precisely the feature that makes the framework distinctive, the rupture, is what prevents it from inheriting flatness for free. As in other cosmological frameworks, the child's flatness requires an additional mechanism, such as a phase of inflation within the child, which the framework is compatible with incorporating but does not generate by itself. This question is developed in more detail among the framework's open problems.

What the framework does genuinely resolve, by contrast, is the horizon problem. The horizon problem is the difficulty of explaining why regions of the universe that could never have been in causal contact present the same microwave background temperature with extraordinary precision. In the BHC framework, all the content of the child universe comes from a single black hole of the parent, and if the state at the moment of rupture is a state of minimum entropy, highly coherent and with all the content in a single causal domain, then the child's homogeneity is a direct consequence of the common origin. Distant regions need not have exchanged information during the expansion: they had the same information from the outset because both come from the same unitary origin. This resolution is robust because it does not depend on flatness or on the value of the cosmological constant: the unitary origin guarantees the child's homogeneity and isotropy regardless of its curvature. Unlike flatness, which the framework cannot inherit from the rupture, homogeneity does follow directly from the common causal origin, and constitutes one of the framework's genuine contributions to the classical problems of cosmology.

Correlation structure of Hawking radiation

If the BHC framework is correct, the correlations of a black hole's Hawking radiation should have a qualitatively different structure from the one standard physics predicts.

In standard physics, Hawking radiation encodes, if information is preserved, the information about the matter that fell directly into the black hole. The Page curve describes how the entropy of the radiation grows to a maximum and subsequently decreases to zero once the black hole has completely evaporated, and the shape of the curve reflects the information about the incident matter.

Under the BHC framework, what Hawking radiation encodes is not simply the matter that fell in, but the complete history of the child universe: its inflation, its structure, its own black holes, its own generations of children, its final dissolution. The Page curve of a BHC black hole should have a structure reflecting the complexity of the interior universe. The relation t_evap ∝ M³, reinterpreted as a regulator of complexity in section 4.10, implies that black holes of very different masses should have qualitatively different Page curves: not simply different temporal scales, but different correlation structures reflecting the differences in the richness of the universes they contain. The nested parentheses of section 4.10 add further structure: each subsequent accretion that generates its own temporal island contributes its own signal to the Hawking radiation, so that the total Page curve of a black hole that has accreted heavily shows a complexity reflecting the sum of all the parentheses.

Hawking radiation has never been detected directly from a real black hole: the temperature of a stellar-mass black hole is of order 10⁻⁸ K, far below any measurable background temperature. The Page curve of a real black hole is not accessible with any current or foreseeable technology. The value of this argument is not observational but theoretical: it establishes that the BHC framework makes a specific prediction differing from standard physics in an aspect that is in principle measurable. A framework making no differentiable prediction would be empirically empty; that it makes a distinguishable one, even if not verifiable in practice, is a positive epistemological property of the framework.

Synthesis of the signatures

The four signatures presented have, taken together, an epistemological value none of them has in isolation. The galactic rotation asymmetry is an observational indication, contested but in principle measurable, connecting with the conservation of angular momentum through the bounce. The coincidence of the radii is an established numerical fact to which the framework gives a structural origin, always with the caution that this is a consistency and not a prediction of the value. The resolution of the horizon problem via the unitary origin is a genuine conceptual contribution, depending on no tuning. And the correlation structure of Hawking radiation is a theoretical discriminator that, though not verifiable, shows that the framework differs from standard physics in aspects that are in principle observable. None of them confirms the framework, and the framework openly acknowledges that flatness, often cited as a strong point of similar proposals, is not something it can claim as an inheritance from the rupture. The honest balance is that the framework makes differentiable predictions and offers differentiable explanations, but that none of them constitutes, as things stand, an empirical confirmation.

4.12 - The unification of the mechanism: rupture energy, inflation, and de Sitter space

This section is the conceptual close of chapter 4. Its purpose is not to introduce new arguments but to identify explicitly the single causal thread running through the entire BHC framework from the parent's collapse to the return of the Hawking radiation. The central argument is the following: the rupture energy, the energy released when the parent's gravity breaks the manifold at the moment of collapse, is the origin, the engine, and the destiny of everything that happens within a child universe. Inflation, expansion, the cosmological constant, the final de Sitter state, and the return to the parent are not separate phenomena requiring independent explanations. They are successive phases of a single process: the complete expression of the rupture energy over the child's lifetime.

The single causal thread

We can trace the thread from beginning to end without any leap or external ingredient:

The parent's gravity compresses matter to the Planck regime. The compression reaches the point where two divergent tendencies (infinite gravity and infinite causal disconnection) cannot coexist within the same manifold. The manifold breaks. The gravitational energy accumulated throughout the collapse is released in a single instant as a kinetic kick in the new manifold.

Within the new manifold, this kick is expressed in temporally separate but causally continuous phases.

The first phase is inflation. In the moment immediately after the bounce, the new manifold is empty, homogeneous, and without structure. The kick acts with no opposition. The result is a rapid, uniform, and isotropic expansion. No independent field is needed for inflation: this is the kinetic kick expressing itself in the absence of resistance. Inflation ends not because a force stops it but because matter begins to form structure. As the primordial fluctuations (inevitable through quantum mechanics in a high-density state) grow and form protogalaxies, matter offers resistance to the expansion and it slows.

The second phase is the standard expansion governed by Λ. The kinetic energy of the kick is distributed between the energy of the matter formed and the residual expansion. Λ is the parametrization of this residual expansion in the language of the Friedmann equations. It appears constant because the kick was a punctual event that has already completed: there is no ongoing physical process feeding it or modifying it. It is the fixed inheritance of a collapse that occurred at the child's Big Bang.

The third phase is terminal de Sitter and the final state. As matter is exhausted (via nuclear fusion, proton decay, Hawking evaporation), the gravitational counterpart of Λ decreases. The feedback mechanism of section 4.6 carries the temporal island towards complete derealization. Λ, which had been in equilibrium with gravity for billions of years, dominates absolutely in the final stages. The child universe reaches state B of the duality of section 4.5: zero density, complete causal isolation, S_external = 0. The kick has been fully expressed.

The fourth phase is the return. The child's final state satisfies the boundary conditions for Hawking emission towards the parent. The child's mass-energy returns to the parent at a rate governed by the thermodynamics of the horizon, as developed in the temporal parenthesis model of section 4.10: the child's information, complete at the boundary from the single instant at which the parenthesis opens, projects onto the parent's temporal axis at the rate the Stefan-Boltzmann law and the Page curve permit. The parenthesis closes. The parent's timeline resumes.

Λ as inheritance of the kick and as an entropic relation

The second phase deserves a clarification, because it connects the physical mechanism of this chapter with the quantitative derivation of the second annex. We have said that Λ is the permanent residue of the kinetic kick. Since the energy of the kick depends on the initial mass of the collapse, Λ is determined by that mass: a property of the parent collapse fixes the child's cosmological constant. This is the mechanism's claim.

The precise functional form of this dependence is the one derived in the second annex from the condition of entropic equality between the horizons, $S_{BH}=S_{dS}$, which gives

$$\Lambda =\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}M^{2}}$$

The kick mechanism and the entropic derivation are therefore not two competing claims about the origin of Λ, but two descriptions of the same fact at different levels. The mechanism establishes that Λ is the energetic inheritance of the collapse and that it must depend on the mass; the entropic derivation fixes its exact functional form. The entropic derivation is, in this sense, the mathematical representation of the function the kick mechanism requires. That two independent arguments — one dynamic and energetic, the other thermodynamic and informational — converge on the same dependence $\Lambda \propto 1/M^{2}$ is a mark of the framework's internal coherence.

This convergence is consistent even at a point that might seem paradoxical. A more energetic kick corresponds to a larger mass, and yet the entropic relation gives a smaller Λ as M grows. There is no contradiction: a larger mass generates a larger horizon, $r_{s}\propto M$, and a larger de Sitter horizon corresponds to a smaller cosmological constant, $\Lambda \propto 1/r_{dS}^{2}$. The more energetic kick translates not into more Λ but into a larger child universe with lower background curvature. This is precisely the duality of contrary singularities of section 4.5, now expressed in energetic terms: more collapse energy, larger horizon, smaller Λ.

It is worth recalling, finally, with the same honesty applied in the second annex, that this relation expresses a structural consistency of the framework with ΛCDM rather than an independent prediction of the value of Λ. Every cosmologically measurable mass already contains Λ through the Friedmann equations, so the relation does not allow the value of Λ to be extracted without prior knowledge of some quantity that already contains it. What the framework establishes is not the value of Λ, but the structure of its dependence on mass and its physical origin as an inheritance of the rupture kick.

Why the unification is significant

Standard cosmology treats inflation, Λ, and the final destiny of the universe as separate problems requiring independent solutions. Inflation requires a field with specific properties not derived from any principle. Λ is a constant inserted into Einstein's equations with no explanation of its origin or its value. The final destiny of the universe is an extrapolation of the standard model towards a remote future that has no causal connection with the origin.

Under the BHC framework, the three problems disappear as independent problems. Inflation is the initial phase of the rupture energy. Λ is the permanent residue of the kinetic kick. The final destiny is the inevitable consequence of Λ dominating over matter that is running out. And the return to the parent closes the energy loop. Four phenomena that in standard cosmology are not causally connected (inflation, Λ, terminal de Sitter, and the evaporation of the black hole in the parent) turn out to be four phases of the same physical process.

The epistemological status of the complete framework

Chapter 4 has developed a theoretical framework that starts from the established physics of ΛCDM and follows it to its logical limits; it reaches conclusions standard cosmology does not predict, but which contradict no established observation. The annex's original proposals (temporal orthogonality, now grounded by elimination in the temporal parenthesis model of section 4.10; derealization; the hierarchy of singularities; the duality of contrary singularities; terminal de Sitter; the cosmological network; entropic accounting; and the genesis mechanism) form a conceptual edifice in which each piece supports the others.

Nevertheless, one must be completely honest about what the framework is and what it is not.

The framework is logically coherent. It starts from well-founded premises. It makes predictions that in principle distinguish it from standard physics. It identifies precisely where current physics is incomplete and what new physics is needed to complete it.

The framework is not a complete physical theory. It has no mathematical formalism of its own. It cannot make precise quantitative predictions beyond the structural relations it has derived. It depends, in order to be formalized, on a theory of quantum gravity that does not exist. Its most original proposals (the rupture of the manifold, dimensional genesis, temporal orthogonality) cannot be derived completely from the existing formalism.

The logical and conceptual framework is complete. Mathematical formalization awaits quantum gravity. Chapter 5, which follows, examines the framework's limitations and tensions with the same precision with which it has been built.

5. Limitations and paradoxes

Chapter 4 built the BHC framework with the same precision with which this chapter subjects it to criticism. The sections that follow examine the tensions, limitations, and open problems of the proposed framework. The aim is not to defend the theory from its weak points but to identify them clearly, distinguishing between tensions that reflect limitations of the approximations used, tensions that reveal genuine open problems, and the fundamental limitation on which the viability of the framework as a whole depends.

5.1 - The three-body paradox in de Sitter space

This section presents a conceptual tension that emerges when the Newtonian criterion of gravitational binding is applied in the regime where the causal structure of de Sitter space is relevant. It is necessary to be precise from the outset about the nature of this tension: it does not demonstrate that ΛCDM is incomplete. Within complete ΛCDM, evolution is deterministic and the field equations determine the outcome. The tension is between two approximation frameworks that coexist within ΛCDM and that, in certain extreme limits, do not produce mutually consistent descriptions.

Consider three gravitational systems treated as point masses: G1, G2, and G3. G1 and G2 are in the marginal situation, with their total mechanical energy close to zero, at the boundary between binding and non-binding. G3 is on a collision trajectory with G2, within the same causal domain. The merger of G2 and G3 would produce a combined system with mass sufficient to bind gravitationally with G1. All three systems lie at distances far below r_dS, so their interaction is causally possible. At the critical moment, when G3 is about to merge with G2, the universe reaches the Λ-dominated regime and approaches the de Sitter state asymptotically.

The tension appears when the Newtonian binding criterion is applied in this marginal regime. The Newtonian criterion treats binding as a binary property determined by the total mechanical energy at a given instant. But in the marginal limit, where the total energy is close to zero, the criterion's answer is extremely sensitive to the exact conditions at the moment it is evaluated. If the criterion is applied before the merger of G2 and G3, G1 and G2 are not bound. If it is applied after the merger, G1 and the combined system would be. In the de Sitter regime, as perturbations grow more slowly and structure freezes asymptotically, the temporal window within which the merger can occur and change the outcome narrows progressively. In the asymptotic limit, the question "did the merger happen in time?" may have no well-defined answer within the Newtonian criterion.

It is necessary to be precise about what this tension demonstrates and what it does not. What it demonstrates is that the Newtonian binding criterion, a useful approximation for most scenarios, has inherent limitations in the marginal limits coinciding with the de Sitter transition. It is not an adequate tool for deciding dynamical outcomes in these extreme cases. The consistent answer within ΛCDM is to abandon the approximate criterion and solve the field equations directly. What it does not demonstrate is that ΛCDM is incomplete or inconsistent: within ΛCDM, evolution is deterministic and the outcome is determined by the field equations, not by the Newtonian criterion.

This tension is relevant to the annex's framework for two reasons. The first is that the Newtonian binding criterion is used to derive r_c and to establish the conditions of the temporal islands. The criterion's limitations in the marginal limits directly affect the applicability of these derivations, and it must be acknowledged that temporal islands, as they have been defined, share the limitations inherent in the criterion from which they derive. The second is that the tension illustrates a real problem of the causal structure of de Sitter space that the annex explores: in the asymptotic de Sitter regime, dynamical processes requiring a well-defined temporal sequence become progressively indeterminate. This is not an incompleteness of ΛCDM but a real property of its causal structure that standard cosmology generally does not develop in detail.

The honest conclusion is the following: the three-body paradox, correctly reformulated, is not a fundamental contradiction of the framework. It shows two things. First, that the Newtonian binding criterion has limitations in the marginal regimes coinciding with the de Sitter transition. Second, that the asymptotic causal structure of this space makes questions requiring precise temporal ordering progressively indeterminate.

5.2 - Observer dependence and causal ontology

A natural response to the three-body paradox is to try to resolve it by appealing to observer dependence, the well-established principle in relativity that different observers can have valid and mutually inconsistent descriptions of the same phenomenon. It might seem that, by assigning different outcomes to different observers, the paradox dissolves. This section argues that it does not.

Observer dependence works as a resolution when the discrepancy between observers is about the description of a phenomenon — when the phenomenon occurs in both cases and simply appears differently from different perspectives. In special relativity, two observers will disagree about the simultaneity of events, but the events occur in both cases. Observer dependence does not change which events occur: it changes how they are ordered or measured.

In the three-body paradox, the discrepancy is not about the description of a phenomenon but about whether a specific physical phenomenon occurs or does not occur. The question is: do the systems bind gravitationally or not? This is a question about the existence of a physical state, not about its measurement. There is no coordinate transformation that can turn "bound" into "unbound": binding is an objective state of spacetime. Therefore, assigning "bound" to one observer and "unbound" to another does not resolve the paradox. It duplicates it. We have two observers with contradictory claims about the same physical reality, and no mechanism explaining by what principle each observer obtains their result.

This conclusion might seem to be in tension with other points of the framework, where observer dependence is indeed invoked as an explanatory resource: entropy and probability as relational properties (section 4.4), causal reality as dependent on the observer's domain (section 4.2), or the complementarity between the parent's and child's descriptions (sections 4.5 and 4.8). Why is observer dependence legitimate in those cases and not in this one? The answer lies in a distinction that must be made explicit.

Observer dependence is valid for properties that are intrinsically relational — that is, properties that are not absolute properties of the system but of the relationship between the system and whoever observes it. Entropy is one, because it measures the microstates accessible to a given observer; causal reality is one, because it describes what can affect what from a given causal domain; simultaneity is one, because it depends on the reference frame. In all these cases, the phenomenon occurs and observer dependence only affects how it is described or which aspects are accessible. Gravitational binding, by contrast, is not a relational property but an objective dynamical fact: a system's total mechanical energy is negative or it is not, and this condition determines whether the system binds or not regardless of who observes it. A state of binding cannot be converted into one of non-binding by any change of perspective, because it is not a property of the perspective. Observer dependence, then, does not apply selectively as convenient: it applies to relational properties and not to objective ones, and gravitational binding belongs to the second category. It is precisely for this reason that assigning different outcomes to different observers does not resolve the paradox but duplicates it.

There is a further difficulty. At the intersection of multiple singularities, as occurs in the de Sitter limit with marginal systems, the observer itself ceases to be a well-defined concept. An observer in physics is a reference system with a defined temporal trajectory, measuring instruments, and the capacity to record events. At the extreme causal boundaries, where time loses operational definition and light cones deform to the point of collapse, there is no well-defined temporal trajectory from which to measure anything. The precondition for observer dependence, that there be well-defined observers, is not met.

Observer dependence is a powerful tool for scenarios where the phenomenon is definite and the discrepancy is about its description. It is not an adequate tool for scenarios where the phenomenon itself is indeterminate and where the observers themselves are not well defined. The three-body paradox belongs to this second type, and therefore observer dependence does not resolve it.

5.3 - Energy conservation across the rupture of the manifold

This is the most fundamental limitation of the BHC framework. The entire genesis mechanism (the quantum bounce, the transmission of the kick energy, the relationship between the child's Λ and the parent's mass) depends on an unprovable premise: that energy is conserved across the rupture of the manifold.

The problem is simple. The conservation of energy is a fundamental principle of physics, but it is always a claim within a system: the energy of a closed system does not change over time. To define energy conservation, a common temporal frame is needed that allows the system's energy at one instant to be compared with its energy at another.

When the manifold breaks and a new manifold is instantiated, there is no common temporal frame between parent and child. The parent has its own time. The child has its own independent time. There is no "instant of the collapse" that is simultaneous from both perspectives, because simultaneity requires a shared temporal frame that does not exist across the rupture. Therefore the question "is the energy the parent loses at collapse equal to the energy the child gains?" has no answer within the current framework. It is not that the answer is negative, but that the question cannot be posed rigorously without a frame connecting the two times. Energy conservation across the rupture is a claim about quantities belonging to causally disconnected and temporally independent manifolds, and there is no established principle guaranteeing their equality.

The implications of this limitation are direct. Without energy conservation across the rupture, the relationship between the child's Λ and the parent's mass loses its foundation: if there is no conservation of energy across the rupture, there is no reason why the child's Λ should bear any specific relation to the parent's mass. The cosmological network ceases to be energetically closed in any precise sense. The single-impulse model loses its quantitative basis.

The annex treats energy conservation across the rupture as a working assumption: a plausible hypothesis the framework requires, but cannot demonstrate. The plausibility argument is the quantum bounce: if the accumulated gravitational energy is what produces the bounce, it is reasonable to suppose that it is conserved through it. But "reasonable" is not "demonstrated".

The formalization of this assumption requires a theory of quantum gravity describing the behaviour of conserved quantities across a rupture of the manifold. Until such a theory exists, energy conservation across the rupture is the point of the framework that requires a physically motivated but undemonstrated act of faith.

It must be made explicit: this is one of the framework's two fundamental limitations. The other, conceptual rather than quantitative in nature, is the dependence on the ontological reading of causal separation, examined in section 5.7. They are independent: a future theory could demonstrate energy conservation across the rupture without pronouncing on the ontological status of causal separation, and vice versa. Most of the framework's other limitations derive from these two or depend partly on them. A theory of quantum gravity demonstrating energy conservation across the rupture would resolve one of the two, and with it a good part of this thesis's open quantitative problems, but would leave the ontological question intact, as it belongs to a different order.

5.4 - The problem of temporal synchronization between parent and child

The temporal parenthesis model developed in section 4.10 connects two quantities that both depend on the mass of the parent black hole: the evaporation time in the parent, which scales as t_evap ∝ M³, and the duration of the child universe's life along its own temporal axis. The natural temptation, and the naive reading to be avoided, is to interpret the former as the measure, from the parent's perspective, of the latter, so that the evaporation time would be the direct projection of the child's life onto the parent's axis. This section examines why this reading does not hold, justifies the resolution section 4.10 already adopts, and acknowledges its cost.

The tension is the following. The framework holds that the temporal axes of parent and child are independent (section 4.1), and section 5.3 has established that no common temporal frame exists allowing time intervals to be compared between the two domains. If that is so, why should the time the child takes to reach its final state, determined by its own internal dynamics and by the feedback of section 4.6, coincide — even in simple measurable proportionality — with the evaporation time observed by the parent? Without a shared temporal frame, there is no principled reason why the two processes should maintain an instant-by-instant correspondence. The naive reading, which would treat t_evap as the duration of the child's life, presupposes precisely the common temporal frame the framework denies.

Three ways of confronting this tension can be conceived, and it is worth setting out all three in order to justify the one the framework adopts.

The first would consist in abandoning the strict independence of the temporal axes. If the parent's and child's times were not completely independent but some residual relation existed between them, a direct temporal correspondence could be derived. This route would resolve the tension, but at a high cost: it would require reformulating the temporal parenthesis model and giving up the causal independence the framework preserves as a central property. It is, moreover, the route the whole elimination argument of section 4.10 has already rejected, by showing that both instant-by-instant correspondence and the variable dilation coefficient are untenable.

The second would consist in accepting the connection as an additional hypothesis, postulating by operational definition that the evaporation time is the duration of the child's life measured in the parent's time. This position would be consistent with the fact that both processes depend on the same mass M, but it would not be derived from the framework's principles, and the legitimate criticism is that it begs the question: it assumes precisely what would need to be justified.

The third route, which is the one section 4.10 adopts, consists in dissolving the problem through observer dependence. The question "how long does the child take to reach its final state, measured in the parent's time?" presupposes that a common answer exists for the two reference frames. But if the temporal axes are genuinely independent, this presupposition is false, and the question itself is ill-formed. It is not that it has an answer hard to obtain: it admits no answer, because it asks to measure an interval of one axis in units of another that does not project onto it. The two descriptions, the parent's and the child's, are complete and valid within their own frames, with neither having to measure anything about the other.

From the parent, the black hole interior is temporally null in the sense that nothing occurs along its temporal axis. What is measured is a mass frozen at the horizon, in the same sense the frozen star problem of section 4.10 establishes: for the external observer, matter never crosses the horizon, and therefore its interior life does not occur at any measurable instant of the parent's time. From this frame, there is only a mass emitting Hawking radiation for a time t_evap; there is no history of the child that needs synchronizing with anything, because from the parent that history is operationally absent. From the child's internal frame, by contrast, the parenthesis opens and the child universe lives its complete life along its own axis. This history is as real as the parent's, but it occurs in a frame the parent cannot traverse. It is not simultaneous with the evaporation, nor does it last the same, nor is it a fraction of it: these categories do not apply, because they would presuppose a common clock that does not exist.

This route introduces no new postulate. It is anchored in two principles the framework already uses: observer dependence in relativity and black hole complementarity from section 3.5. It is the same argument that resolves the frozen star problem in section 4.10 and that answers the criticism about the mass of the nested parentheses. Parent and child are not two observers of the same process, but two observers in causally separated frames, each with a complete description of their own reality. This is why section 4.10 no longer presents t_evap as a measure of the child's duration, but explicitly distinguishes between correlation through common dependence and direct measurement.

This resolution has a cost that must be acknowledged openly. The reading according to which M would be a regulator of complexity in a direct temporal sense — that is, that the evaporation time would be the measure of the richness of the child's life projected onto the parent's axis — must be abandoned. What can be claimed rigorously is weaker but remains significant: M simultaneously determines two independent consequences occurring in different frames. In the parent, it determines the evaporation time. In the child, it determines the initial energy available and, through it, the capacity for complexity of its history. The two consequences depend on the same parameter, but they are not synchronized: they are separate manifestations of M in two frames that do not measure one another. The child's complexity and the parent's evaporation time scale with M for different reasons, not because one measures the other.

With this reformulation, M remains the parameter governing both the child's richness and the observable evaporation time, but it ceases to be a regulator of complexity in the direct temporal sense a naive reading would suggest. The framework loses predictive capacity about the comparative duration of the processes, and gains internal coherence: it does not claim to synchronize what cannot be synchronized.

It is worth placing the status of the three routes precisely. The framework does not need to decide between them as if the question remained open, because section 4.10 has already adopted the third as the coherent reading of the temporal parenthesis model, and has done so without postulating any additional connection or abandoning the independence of the axes. The first two routes remain as alternatives a future theory of quantum gravity could explore, if it came to establish some formal relation between the axes across the rupture, but they are not necessary within the current framework. Meanwhile, the scaling t_evap ∝ M³ is used with a precise and limited scope: it serves to calculate the black hole's evaporation time, which is a real observable from the parent's frame, but not to infer the duration of the child's life in any comparable sense. The relation expresses the common dependence of two processes in independent frames, not a correspondence between them.

5.5 - Preservation of entanglement across the rupture of the manifold

The previous sections have identified four structural tensions of the framework, and this section addresses a fifth, of a different order: it is not a tension between approximate descriptions, nor a limitation of the conceptual apparatus, but a question the framework generates directly about its own internal structure. If the interior of a black hole is a manifold causally independent of the parent, how can the quantum entanglement structure of the Hawking mechanism be maintained across that rupture?

This section presents first the problem, then a line of approach based on an extension of the principle of complementarity, and finally the tensions this line generates with other parts of the framework. None of the arguments that follow is presented as a resolution: the section documents a state of thinking, not a closed result.

The Hawking mechanism and the entanglement structure

Hawking radiation, as set out in section 2.3, emerges from the behaviour of the quantum vacuum field in the presence of an event horizon. The usual heuristic presentation speaks of virtual particle-antiparticle pairs created near the horizon, one of which escapes with positive energy while the other falls inwards with negative energy. This image is useful for intuition, but does not correspond exactly to the rigorous calculation.

The technical formulation of the mechanism is based on the Bogoliubov transformation between the modes of the asymptotic vacuum in the past and those of the asymptotic vacuum in the future. The presence of the horizon makes these two bases fail to coincide, and the result is that the asymptotic vacuum in the past, seen from the future, contains a thermal distribution of modes. There are not, strictly speaking, virtual particles falling in: there is a restructuring of the vacuum that, measured from far away, takes the form of an emission of radiation with a thermal spectrum.

The aspect relevant to this section is the following: the quantum state emerging from this restructuring is not a separable product. The exterior modes, which constitute the observable Hawking radiation, and the interior modes, corresponding to the field inside the horizon, form an entangled state. This entanglement structure is essential for the mechanism to be consistent with quantum unitarity: without it, the emitted radiation would be purely thermal and could not encode information about the black hole's initial state, which would lead directly to the Hawking information paradox, discussed in the context of complementarity in section 3.5 and taken up again in section 4.8.

Under standard cosmology, this entanglement structure is mathematically delicate but conceptually clear: the interior and exterior modes belong to the same spacetime manifold, and the entangled state can be formulated rigorously within quantum field theory in curved space. The modes are causally separated by the horizon, but they share the same global temporal frame and the same Hilbert structure.

The tension the rupture of the manifold generates

Under the BHC framework the annex develops, the situation changes fundamentally. The black hole interior is not a causally inaccessible region of a single manifold shared with the exterior: it is a genuinely separate manifold, with its own temporal axis orthogonal to the parent's (section 4.1), its own autonomous evolution (section 4.10), and its own causal structure (section 4.9). The dimensional rupture separates the two regions completely, not partially.

This separation generates a question the framework had not addressed explicitly in the previous sections. Where does the entanglement structure of the Hawking pair live? If the interior modes now belong to a manifold separate from the parent, and the exterior modes belong to the parent's manifold, the entangled state seems to require a connection between two manifolds that, by construction, have no common causal frame. Put another way: entanglement is a quantum correlation between the states of subsystems, and this correlation requires some sense in which the two subsystems belong to one and the same global system. If the two manifolds are genuinely separate, in what way can we say the correlation continues to exist?

This tension is not a problem inherited from standard physics. It is a tension the BHC framework generates on its own through its structure. Under ΛCDM without rupture of the manifold, the question has no content: interior and exterior modes live in the same manifold and entanglement is a well-defined structure within quantum field theory in curved space. Under this thesis's framework, the question does have content, and must be addressed.

There are three possible answers a priori. The first is that entanglement breaks at the rupture, so that the exterior modes are left in a purely thermal mixed state in the parent and the interior modes in an independent state in the child. The second is that entanglement is preserved in some formal way depending on the physics of the rupture, not yet specified. The third is that the question itself is ill-posed, and that the notion of entanglement between two causally separated manifolds has no invariant meaning.

The annex cannot decide between these three possibilities with current means. But it can explore a line of approach that, if coherent, suggests a way of thinking the problem without having to break either unitarity or the structure of the framework.

Complementarity at the scale of the global quantum state

Section 3.5 introduces the principle of black hole complementarity as one of this thesis's theoretical precedents. This principle, formulated by Susskind, Thorlacius, and Uglum, establishes that an external observer and a freely falling observer can have mutually exclusive but individually consistent descriptions of the same physical phenomenon. The external observer sees a black hole with Hawking radiation emerging and matter accumulating asymptotically at the horizon; the freely falling observer crosses the horizon without detecting anything particular and continues on to the singularity. The two descriptions are incompatible if one tries to unify them, but no physical operation can compare them directly because the two observers are separated by the horizon. Complementarity is therefore a form of duality: two descriptions of the same quantum state, valid in mutually inaccessible causal domains.

The annex proposes extending this principle to the problem of entanglement across the rupture. The proposal can be formulated thus: there are not two different quantum states that must be connected across the rupture. There is a single quantum state admitting two complementary descriptions according to the manifold from which it is observed.

From the parent's manifold, the state is described as Hawking radiation emerging from the horizon, with an entanglement structure between exterior modes and formally interior modes. The interior branch is not causally accessible, but its presence within the global state is necessary to preserve the unitarity of the exterior description. From the parent, the black hole is a thermal object with a well-defined Bekenstein-Hawking entropy, and the emitted radiation encodes, in its fine structure, the information about the interior state.

From the child's manifold, the same state is described as interior field modes forming part of the child's material content from its beginning. These modes evolve along the child's temporal axis, participate in its internal dynamics, and will end up derealizing progressively as the child reaches its final state (section 4.6). From the child, there is no external Hawking radiation to detect: the interior modes are simply part of the quantum field of the child's domain, with their own evolutionary history.

The key proposal is that these two descriptions are not of different things. There is no pair of entangled particles with one branch in each manifold. There is a single quantum pair state, which from one causal foliation is described as emergent radiation with an inaccessible interior branch, and from the other is described as field modes evolving in an independent temporal domain. The two descriptions are complementary in Susskind's exact sense: they are valid in mutually inaccessible causal domains, and no physical operation can compare them directly because no observer has access to both manifolds simultaneously.

Under this reading, the tension of entanglement across the rupture is not a paradox to be resolved, but a direct consequence of trying to apply a global picture — the two manifolds as parts of a larger system — to a situation where the global frame does not exist. The entangled state is a property of the total quantum system, and this total system has no privileged foliation. The two descriptions, the parent's and the child's, are the only ones possible, and neither can claim to be the complete description without complementarity with the other.

The identification must be at the scale of the global quantum state

It is necessary to be very precise about what this proposal claims and what it does not, because a naive formulation could lead to serious contradictions with well-established principles.

The intuitive temptation is to say that a particle of the parent is the same as a particle of the child. This formulation is problematic for two technical reasons.

The first is quantum indistinguishability. In quantum mechanics, particles of the same type are indistinguishable in a very strong sense: there is no physical fact allowing them to be identified individually within a multiparticle state. This is not a limitation of measurement, but a structural property of the formalism, with real experimental consequences in quantum statistics. Two field modes with the same quantum numbers but at different positions are indistinguishable as a type but different as states: they are two modes, not a single mode in two places. Treating them as the same particle in two manifolds would require an argument that cannot be made by appealing only to the identity of the quantum numbers.

The second is charge conservation. In the Hawking mechanism, the pair produced at the horizon is particle-antiparticle: by conservation of electric charge, baryon number, lepton number, and any other conserved charge, the two members of the pair must have opposite charges. If one said that the parent's particle, the one that escapes, is the same as the child's particle, the one that falls in, the identification would give two descriptions of a single particle with the same charge, and the pair would disappear as a pair. This would violate charge conservation flagrantly, and charge conservation is a direct consequence of fundamental symmetries of the Standard Model that no speculative framework should break lightly.

For these two reasons, the proposal cannot be formulated in terms of a single individual particle. The object admitting the two complementary descriptions is the global quantum state, not its constituents. The entangled pair state has total charge zero, because the two branches have opposite charges and conservation is maintained automatically within the global state, and it admits a description from the parent, where one branch is observable as radiation and the other is formally interior, and a description from the child, where the branch the parent sees as interior is simply a mode of the child's field, and the branch the parent sees as radiation is causally inaccessible.

This reformulation preserves charge conservation trivially, because charge is a property of the global state that remains invariant under any change of foliation. It also fits with how Susskind's complementarity is applied in the established literature: what is the same under complementarity is not an individual particle, but a region of spacetime or a quantum state admitting multiple mutually exclusive descriptions.

The precise formulation of the proposal would therefore be the following: the quantum state emerging from the Hawking mechanism at a black hole's horizon is a single entity admitting two complementary descriptions. One from the parent's manifold, where the state takes the form of a pair entangled between exterior modes, observable as radiation, and formally interior modes, causally inaccessible. The other from the child's manifold, where the same state takes the form of field modes forming part of the child's initial content and evolving along its temporal axis. The two descriptions are valid within their respective causal domains, and cannot be compared directly because no observer has simultaneous access to both manifolds.

Tension with section 5.4

The proposal just presented is coherent with the principle of complementarity and with the general structure of the framework, but it generates a direct tension with section 5.4 that must be acknowledged explicitly.

Section 5.4 establishes that no common temporal frame exists between the parent's manifold and the child's, and that therefore the question of which event of the parent coincides with which event of the child has no answer within the current framework. This absence of a common temporal frame is a limitation affecting the formulation of energy conservation (section 5.3), the synchronization between the black hole's evaporation in the parent and the child's life (section 5.4), and any other quantity requiring states at corresponding instants of the two manifolds to be compared.

The proposal of complementarity at the global scale implicitly requires some notion of correspondence between the states of parent and child. When we say the same quantum state admits two descriptions, we claim there is an entity, the global state, that can be described from two different perspectives. But for this entity to be coherent, the two descriptions must be compatible in some sense, and compatibility seems to require being able to compare the state in the parent with the state in the child at corresponding moments. Without a common temporal frame, this comparison is not well defined.

There are three possible ways of addressing this tension, each with its own implications.

The first is to accept that the proposal requires the existence of some common temporal structure, even if implicit, and to conclude that this thesis's framework needs to be extended to include it. This would weaken the strict independence of the temporal axes established in section 4.1 and would accept that the rupture of the manifold is not absolute in every sense.

The second is to formulate the proposal in terms not requiring instant-by-instant correspondence between the two manifolds. In this case, the same quantum entity would be defined structurally rather than temporally: as an entity characterized by its quantum invariants — the conserved charges, the entanglement correlations, and the spectral structure — which are the same in both descriptions, without the states needing to be synchronized at any instant. This formulation is closer to how invariants are thought of in quantum field theory in curved space, where many physically significant objects are foliation-invariant quantities. It is, of the three, the most coherent with the resolution section 5.4 already adopts, where the relationship between parent and child is described in terms of common dependence on mass and not of temporal correspondence: the global quantum entity would be, in this sense, an invariant shared by the two descriptions, not something that must be synchronized between them.

The third is to acknowledge that the proposal is incomplete until a theory of quantum gravity exists that includes descriptions independent of a temporal background. Some approaches to quantum gravity, such as canonical formulations based on the Wheeler-DeWitt equation or covariant formulations based on spin foams, attempt precisely to build gravitational quantum states without a privileged background time. If one of these approaches succeeded, it might provide the language needed to formulate complementarity without a common temporal frame.

The annex acknowledges that none of the three routes is fully satisfactory within the current framework. The first weakens fundamental principles of the framework; the second requires a formalization the annex cannot develop with current tools, although it is the direction most consistent with the rest of the framework; the third rests on physics that does not yet exist. The complementarity proposal is coherent with the framework at its conceptual level, but its rigorous formalization requires advances the annex cannot anticipate.

Relationship with the Hawking information problem

Section 4.8 establishes that the framework is compatible with three possible resolutions of the Hawking information problem: unitary preservation, thermalization with loss of information, and reset through rupture of the manifold. That section adopts preservation as a working hypothesis, as the only one compatible with the premise of conservation of information the framework has already assumed, but acknowledges that it cannot definitively rule out the other two with its current tools.

This section's proposal adds an important nuance to that discussion. If complementarity at the global scale is correct, then the Hawking information problem may not have the form usually attributed to it. The standard question is whether information is conserved or lost when the black hole evaporates completely, and the three standard answers assume the question has an invariant answer.

Under complementarity, the question could be reformulated thus: if the quantum state has two complementary descriptions, the conservation or loss of information depends on which description is adopted. From the parent, the state is described as emergent radiation with an inaccessible interior branch, and the information appears encoded in the correlation structure of the radiation; this is precisely the description under which section 4.8 adopts preservation and the rate of return is obtained from the Page curve. From the child, the same state is described as field modes forming part of its content, and the information flows with its internal evolution up to the final derealization. The two descriptions do not contradict each other: they are complementary.

This suggests a possible fourth reading of the information problem, which does not replace the working hypothesis of 4.8 but places it in perspective: the preservation the framework adopts is the correct description from the parent's foliation, which is the relevant one for the physical observable, the Hawking radiation; but the question of whether information is conserved in an absolute, foliation-invariant sense presupposes a global frame in which the two descriptions can be compared, and this frame does not exist. Information is conserved within each description, and in particular is recovered through the Page curve within the parent's description; its relationship between descriptions is what remains without an invariant answer within the current framework. The fourth reading therefore does not contradict the adoption of preservation made in section 4.8, but makes its scope precise: preservation is a property of the parent's description, not necessarily a global invariant.

This fourth reading is compatible with this thesis's framework and coherent with this section's complementarity proposal. It does not resolve the information problem in any absolute sense, but reformulates it so that its apparent paradox becomes a natural property of a framework without a privileged foliation.

Epistemological status

This section must close with the same honesty with which it opened. The proposal presented is not a resolution of the problem of entanglement across the rupture of the manifold. It is a line of approach, coherent with the principle of complementarity already present in the framework, suggesting a way of thinking the problem without having to break either quantum unitarity or the causal structure of the framework.

The proposal has three virtues. First, it aligns with a principle, complementarity, already accepted in the established black hole literature, and therefore requires no new mechanisms to be postulated. Second, it automatically preserves charge conservation and other conserved quantities, because these are properties of the global state that do not depend on the foliation. Third, it suggests a possible reformulation of the Hawking information problem that is compatible with this thesis's framework and that makes precise the scope of the working hypothesis adopted in section 4.8.

The proposal also has three important weaknesses. First, it generates a direct tension with section 5.4 on temporal synchronization, and resolving that tension requires either weakening principles of the framework or a formalization the annex cannot develop, although the route of foliation invariants points in a direction compatible with the rest of the framework. Second, it extends the principle of complementarity to a domain — between manifolds causally separated by a dimensional rupture — where its applicability is not established by any known result: it is an extrapolation, not a consequence. Third, its complete formalization depends on a theory of quantum gravity including descriptions independent of a temporal background, and this theory does not yet exist.

Taken together, the proposal is presented as a coherent conjecture within the framework, not as an established derivation or a closed resolution. It fits the pattern the whole of chapter 4 has followed: identify where current physics is incomplete, propose a possible direction of approach, and acknowledge explicitly what remains to be resolved. As with all of this thesis's proposals, its value lies not in closing the problem but in making it visible and suggesting where to look in order to begin answering it.

The tension of entanglement across the rupture of the manifold is perhaps one of the most interesting questions the framework generates. It is a question standard physics cannot pose, because it does not postulate the rupture of the manifold, and it is a question this thesis cannot resolve, precisely because its resolution requires physics that does not yet exist. This asymmetry, between the capacity to pose questions and the capacity to answer them, is a structural feature of the framework: it is not a defect of the thesis, but a natural consequence of a conceptual framework that goes beyond what the current formalism can capture.

5.6 - Open problems and necessary physics

This section sets out systematically the unresolved dependencies of the BHC framework. The aim is twofold: to be honest about what the theory cannot claim, and to identify precisely what new physics is needed to complete it.

The first open problem is the formalization of the hierarchy of singularities. The hierarchy identified in section 4.3 (representational at r_c, curvature singularities inside black holes, causal at the de Sitter scale) is a conceptual instrument, not a formalized theorem. The formal relation between the three levels, and especially the claim that each level contains the lower ones and determines their physical relevance, requires a consistent mathematical framework treating all three simultaneously. This framework does not exist.

The second is the Hawking information problem. As established in section 4.8, the annex is compatible with the three possible resolutions (preservation, thermalization, reset through rupture), and adopts the preservation of information as a working hypothesis, as the only one compatible with the premise of conservation of information the framework has already assumed and on which the entropic derivation of the second annex rests. This adoption, however, is not equivalent to a demonstration: the framework cannot formally rule out thermalization or reset with current tools. The definitive resolution of this problem would determine the exact nature of the entropic accounting between universes and would complete the argument of the network. Until it exists, the annex's entropic accounting is correct in its general outlines, and operationally consistent under the preservation hypothesis, but incomplete in its details.

The third is the arrow of time between generations. The black holes generating child universes form in a high-entropy universe. How can a high-entropy black hole produce a low-entropy child? The annex's partial answer is that entropy is a property of the manifold and not of the particles (section 4.8), so that the child begins with its own entropic counter. This relational reading has a geometric correlate developed in the second annex, and it is worth formulating it with the same caution. In the language of curvature, the fact that the child begins its entropic counter from the minimum of its frame manifests as a vanishing Weyl tensor at the child's beginning.

The Weyl tensor encodes the free curvature of spacetime, the part of the geometry not forced directly by matter, and is the natural measure of gravitational entropy in Penrose's sense. From the child's foliation, this free curvature is minimal, while the Ricci tensor, coupled to the matter-energy density, carries the content the child inherits.

It must be stressed that this vanishing Weyl is a property of the description from the child, not a claim that the child objectively has less gravitational entropy than the parent in absolute terms: it is the minimum of the child's frame, coherent with the global conservation of information. The two descriptions — the loss of the parent's accessible phase space and the erasure of the Weyl tensor from the child's foliation — are the same relational fact seen at two levels, the thermodynamic-statistical and the geometric.

The fourth is the treatment of extended mass distributions at causal boundaries. The three-body paradox (section 5.1), apparent but revealing, is a concrete case of a more general problem: the annex treats gravitational systems as point masses in order to derive r_c and r_dS, but this idealization fails at the limits where binding decisions occur. A complete theory would have to treat extended mass distributions in the presence of causal horizons consistently.

The fifth is the threshold between the primary child universe and nested parentheses. The temporal parenthesis model (section 4.10) establishes that matter accreted later forms independent nested parentheses, but does not specify the minimum mass threshold for a nested parenthesis: whether every constituent crossing the horizon generates its own independent temporal domain, or whether there is a minimum threshold of mass or energy. Without this threshold, the model is qualitative but not quantitative.

The sixth is energy conservation across the rupture, established in section 5.3 as one of the framework's two fundamental limitations. This limitation is quantitative in nature: without a common temporal frame between parent and child, the equality between the energy the parent loses and the energy the child receives cannot be formulated rigorously. Most of the other open problems are simplified or partly resolved if this problem has a solution. The second fundamental limitation, conceptual in nature and independent of this one, is the framework's dependence on the ontological reading of causal separation, developed in section 5.7.

The seventh is precisely that ontological dependence. The entire conceptual edifice of the framework (derealization, temporal islands, terminal de Sitter, the duality of singularities) rests on taking the permanent causal separation at r_dS as a genuine ontological difference and not as a merely practical impossibility of communication. This is an interpretive choice, marked as such in section 4.2, that the framework cannot demonstrate with current tools. If the alternative reading were correct, a good part of the framework's conceptual structure would lose its foundation. Because of its centrality, this limitation is treated independently in section 5.7, together with the justification of why the rupture of the manifold is mathematically conceivable in the Planck regime.

The eighth open problem is the origin of the specific value of Λ in our universe. If the child's properties are determined by the parent's (as the deterministic model of section 4.7 contemplates), the concrete value of Λ we observe is an inherited contingent condition this thesis does not claim to explain: it is simply the value corresponding to the mass of the parent black hole that generated our universe, through the relation Λ = 3c⁴/(4G²M²) derived in the second annex. This position is coherent but leaves unexplained why the inherited value turns out to be precisely one of the few compatible with the formation of galaxies and complex structure, a problem known as the fine-tuning of the cosmological constant that affects standard cosmology equally.

If, by contrast, there is variability between generations, Smolin's cosmological natural selection (section 3.3) provides an evolutionary explanation compatible with the framework: universes with values of Λ that permit the formation of many black holes have more descendants, and therefore the constants favouring structure statistically dominate the population of universes. Under this reading, our Λ is not an improbable coincidence but a typical value of a reproductively successful cosmological lineage. This thesis is compatible with this explanation, but does not require it, and as established in section 4.7, the distinction between the deterministic model and the Smolinian model is empirically impossible to verify from within any universe of the network. The fine-tuning problem therefore remains a question the framework can accommodate but cannot resolve on its own.

5.7 - The rupture of the manifold and the ontological dependence

The previous sections have examined concrete tensions of the framework: limitations of approximations, problems of synchronization, questions of entanglement. This section addresses the most fundamental of all, the one the chapter's introduction announced as the limitation on which the viability of the framework as a whole depends. Unlike the energy conservation of section 5.3, which is a limitation of a quantitative nature, the one examined here is conceptual in nature. Both are fundamental, and both are independent: a future theory could resolve one without touching the other. This section develops in two layers. The first justifies why the rupture of the manifold is a mathematically conceivable possibility and not an arbitrary imposition. The second identifies the framework's dependence on the ontological reading of causal separation as the second fundamental limitation.

Why the rupture is mathematically conceivable

The framework proposes that, in the Planck density regime, the manifold does not remain continuous through the collapse but breaks, generating a new causally independent manifold. This proposal is distinguished from continuous-bounce frameworks, where the geometry remains continuous, and it is natural to ask whether the rupture is a serious possibility or simply a hypothesis introduced for convenience. The argument that follows does not demonstrate that the rupture occurs, but shows that it is mathematically admissible in the regime where the framework locates it, which is all the framework needs in order to propose it as a working hypothesis.

The starting point is the behaviour of the field equations at the classical singularity. As established in sections 2.1 and 4.3, when gravitational collapse approaches the singularity, the curvature invariants diverge and Einstein's equations cease to produce physically meaningful results. General relativity does not claim that anything physical diverges at this point: it claims that the theory has reached the limit of its domain of validity. What replaces the singularity is an open question requiring physics beyond classical general relativity.

In this regime, the uniqueness and regularity of solutions cease to be guaranteed. Under normal conditions, the existence and uniqueness theorems ensure that, given initial conditions on a Cauchy hypersurface, the evolution of the metric is uniquely determined. But these theorems presuppose that the curvature remains finite and that the manifold retains its differentiable structure. When the curvature diverges, the theorems' hypotheses cease to hold, and with them the guarantee that the continuation of the manifold is unique or that it exists as a regular manifold. It is precisely at this point where solutions cease to be unique that a non-trivial continuation, such as the rupture into a new manifold, becomes mathematically conceivable. There is no theorem prohibiting it, because the theorems that would govern the continuation have ceased to be applicable.

It is worth stressing carefully what this argument claims and what it does not. It does not claim that the rupture is the only possible continuation, nor that it is the most probable, nor that it derives from any established principle. Standard general relativity, faced with the singularity, simply declares itself incomplete and does not pronounce on what replaces it; it could be replaced by a continuous bounce, a regular quantum gravity structure, or the rupture the framework proposes. What the argument establishes is more modest but sufficient: that the rupture is not excluded by the formalism, because the formalism has ceased to have jurisdiction in this regime. The rupture is one of the continuations the limit of general relativity leaves open, and the framework adopts it because it is the one that gives meaning to temporal orthogonality, to the child's causal disconnection, and to the duality of singularities. Its ultimate justification, like that of any continuation in this regime, requires a theory of quantum gravity that does not yet exist.

To this mathematical possibility is added the symmetry argument developed in section 4.9: if the manifold breaks, the rupture affects the complete manifold and not selectively some coordinates, because a manifold is a unitary mathematical object and a partial rupture would require an asymmetric treatment of the dimensions that no established principle justifies. The conjunction of the two arguments — the loss of uniqueness in the extreme curvature regime and the unitary character of the manifold as an object — is what makes the rupture a coherent hypothesis. Coherent, however, does not mean demonstrated, and this is where the second layer begins.

The dependence on the ontological reading of causal separation

The rupture of the manifold, even accepted as mathematically conceivable, does not by itself suffice to sustain the framework. The framework does not merely claim that the manifold breaks: it claims that the two resulting regions are ontologically independent, that they exist in genuinely separate domains of causal reality. And this second claim, which is what gives content to the whole conceptual edifice, rests on an interpretive reading the framework cannot demonstrate.

It is worth seeing exactly where this reading enters. In section 4.2 it was established, as an original derivative step, that the permanent causal separation occurring when two systems exceed r_dS was to be understood not merely as a practical impossibility of communication, but as an ontological difference: that what is causally real for one system may be causally non-existent for another, without either being mistaken. That reading was explicitly marked there as an interpretation of the author's own, not derived from physics, and it was noted that the following sections depended on it. This section acknowledges the magnitude of that dependence.

Practically the entire conceptual edifice of the framework rests on it. Derealization (section 4.3) is the loss of causal reality of a constituent that exceeds r_dS; if causal separation were only an impossibility of communication and not an ontological difference, derealization would be a loss of contact, not a loss of reality, and the concept would lose its force. Temporal islands (section 4.3) are defined as the only domains where time and causal reality have content; this definition presupposes that outside them causal reality is genuinely absent, not merely inaccessible. Terminal de Sitter (section 4.6) is the state where all matter has lost all causal reality; without the ontological reading, it would be a state where all matter has lost all contact, which is a very different thing. And the duality of contrary singularities (section 4.5) identifies the child's terminal state with the interior of a black hole from the parent's perspective; this identification makes sense because both are situations of ontologically real causal isolation, not of mere incommunication.

The point is that established physics does not compel this reading. What physics establishes is that two systems separated by r_dS can never exchange a signal again: this is a fact about the causal structure of de Sitter space, undisputed. What physics does not establish is whether this impossibility of signalling constitutes a difference in what is real for each system, or simply a practical barrier leaving an underlying shared reality intact. Standard cosmology does not pronounce on this question, because it does not need it for any of its purposes. The BHC framework, by contrast, takes a position, and its entire conceptual structure depends on which position it takes.

This is therefore the second fundamental limitation, independent of the energy conservation of section 5.3. They are independent because they operate on different planes: energy conservation is a quantitative question about whether a quantity is preserved across the rupture, while the ontological reading is a conceptual question about what causal separation means. A theory of quantum gravity could, in principle, demonstrate energy conservation across the rupture without pronouncing at all on the ontological status of causal separation, or vice versa. Neither reduces to the other, and the framework needs both to stand: the first for its quantitative consistency, the second for its conceptual coherence.

The status of the framework in the light of the two limitations

Acknowledging these two fundamental limitations is not an act of rhetorical modesty but a precision about what the framework is. The BHC framework is not a complete physical theory and does not claim to be one. It is a conceptual construction that takes the established physics of ΛCDM and general relativity, follows it to its limits, and proposes a coherent continuation in the regime where those theories declare themselves incomplete. This continuation rests on two hypotheses the current formalism can neither confirm nor refute: that energy is conserved across the rupture, and that causal separation is ontologically real.

Honesty about these two dependencies is, paradoxically, one of the framework's strengths. A framework that hid these hypotheses, or presented them as derived consequences rather than interpretive choices, would be more vulnerable, because an examiner would find them and the framework would not have anticipated the finding. By contrast, a framework that identifies precisely the two points on which its viability rests, that explains why they are reasonable hypotheses without pretending they are demonstrated, and that acknowledges that its formalization awaits physics that does not yet exist, offers the reader exactly what they need in order to evaluate it: not an assertion of certainty, but a precise map of where what can be grounded ends and what must be postulated begins.

6. Glossary

This glossary collects the technical terms the annex presupposes and that are needed to follow the arguments, but that are not developed within the body of the text. It does not include this thesis's own concepts (derealization, temporal islands, duality of contrary singularities, etc.), since these are defined and explained in the corresponding sections of chapter 4. Entries keep the alphabetical order of the Catalan original.

Anti-de Sitter (space): Solution of Einstein's equations with a negative cosmological constant (Λ < 0). The opposite of de Sitter space. The setting of the AdS/CFT correspondence.

Baryonic (matter): Ordinary matter composed of baryons (protons and neutrons) and electrons. It constitutes approximately 5% of the energy content of the universe.

Quantum vacuum: The minimum-energy state of a quantum field. It is not an absolute void: it contains fluctuations of virtual particles permitted by the uncertainty principle.

Light cone: Geometric representation of the set of trajectories light can follow from a point of spacetime. It defines the limits of causality.

Page curve: The curve describing how the entropy of the radiation emitted by a black hole evolves over evaporation: it grows to a maximum around half the hole's lifetime (the Page time) and then decreases to zero as evaporation completes. Under conservation of information, it guarantees that all the information eventually emerges.

Curvature: Measure of how spacetime deviates from flat geometry. In general relativity, curvature is produced by the presence of mass and energy.

Critical density: The energy density for which the spatial geometry of the universe is exactly flat. Defined as ρ_c = 3H₀²/8πG.

Causal domain: Region of spacetime within which events can be connected by signals travelling at speeds equal to or lower than that of light.

Planck scale: Physical regime characterized by the Planck units, where the quantum effects of gravity should be dominant. At distances below 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m or times below 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ s.

Phase space: Mathematical space in which each point represents a possible state of a physical system, encoding all the dynamical variables (position, momentum, etc.).

Spacetime: The four-dimensional geometric structure (three spatial and one temporal) on which the theories of relativity are formulated.

Stochastic: The branch of mathematics that combines probability and statistics to analyse random phenomena, where events depend on chance.

Euclidean (space): Geometric space with positive signature (+,+,+) where all distances are spacelike. It is the geometry of everyday experience.

Scale factor (a(t)): Function describing how cosmological distances change over time. If it grows, the universe expands.

Fuzzball: Proposal within string theory according to which black holes have no central singularity but an extended structure composed of strings.

Geodesic: The straightest possible path between two points of a curved space. In general relativity, free particles follow geodesics.

Quantum gravity: Hypothetical theory that would unify general relativity with quantum mechanics. No accepted formulation exists.

Loop Quantum Gravity: Proposal for quantum gravity that quantizes spacetime itself into discrete units.

Helicity: Property of a system reflecting a preference of rotation or direction in its global behaviour.

Homogeneity: Property of a system according to which its characteristics are the same at every point. A fundamental principle of standard cosmology.

Isotropy: Property of a system according to which it has no privileged direction. Together with homogeneity, it forms the cosmological principle.

Lorentzian (spacetime): Spacetime with signature (−,+,+,+), where the temporal term has the opposite sign to the spatial terms. The fundamental geometry of relativity.

Macrostate: State of a system described by a small set of global variables (temperature, pressure, volume). A macrostate is compatible with many microstates.

Megaparsec (Mpc): Astronomical unit of distance equivalent to a million parsecs, approximately 3.26 million light years. Used to measure intergalactic distances.

Metric: Mathematical object defining the distances between points in a space or spacetime. In relativity, the metric encodes the geometry of the system.

Microstate: Specific microscopic configuration of a system compatible with a given macroscopic state. The number of microstates determines the entropy.

Angular momentum: Physical quantity measuring the rotation of a system. It is conserved in the absence of external torques.

Normalization: Mathematical condition according to which the sum (or integral) of a probability distribution must be exactly 1.

Ontology: In philosophy, the study of what exists and how it exists. In this annex, it is used to distinguish between real properties and merely descriptive ones.

Parsec: Astronomical unit of distance equivalent to approximately 3.26 light years.

Cosmological principle: Hypothesis according to which, on sufficiently large scales, the universe is homogeneous and isotropic.

Hawking radiation: Thermal radiation emitted by a black hole at temperature T = ħc³/8πGMk_B, inversely proportional to its mass. It does not come from particles crossing the horizon, but from the restructuring of the quantum vacuum in the presence of an event horizon. The radiated power scales as M⁻², so the evaporation time grows as M³.

Rapidity (φ): Parameter quantifying the tilt between temporal axes in special relativity, defined by tanh(φ) = v/c. Unlike velocity, rapidities add additively.

Redshift: Lengthening of the wavelength of light caused by the expansion of the universe or by objects receding. It allows the recession velocity of galaxies to be measured.

Signature (of a metric): Pattern of signs of the diagonal terms of the metric. Euclidean spaces have signature (+,+,+); Lorentzian spacetimes have signature (−,+,+,+) or (+,−,−,−).

Simultaneity: Temporal coincidence of two events. In relativity, simultaneity depends on the observer.

Quantum superposition: Property of quantum mechanics according to which a system can exist in a linear combination of multiple states simultaneously until a measurement is made.

Shell theorem: Result according to which a spherically symmetric body exerts, outside its radius, the same gravitational attraction as if all its mass were concentrated at the centre.

Tensor: Mathematical object generalizing scalars, vectors, and matrices. Tensors are fundamental for formulating the laws of physics independently of the coordinate system.

Torsion: Geometric property measuring how a spacetime "twists" along a trajectory. Absent in standard general relativity, present in Einstein-Cartan theory.

Planck unit: Natural unit derived from the fundamental constants (G, ħ, c). Planck length ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m, Planck time ≈ 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ s, Planck mass ≈ 2.2 × 10⁻⁸ kg.

Manifold: Mathematical object generalizing the notion of a surface to any number of dimensions. A spacetime is a four-dimensional differentiable manifold.

Spacelike vector: Vector in Lorentzian spacetime with positive scalar product. It connects causally separated events, which cannot influence one another.

Timelike vector: Vector in Lorentzian spacetime with negative scalar product. It connects events that can be causally related.

Weyl (tensor): The part of spacetime curvature not forced directly by the matter present — the free curvature — unlike the Ricci tensor, which is coupled to the matter-energy density. Penrose proposes it as the natural measure of gravitational entropy: a vanishing Weyl tensor corresponds to minimal gravitational entropy.
