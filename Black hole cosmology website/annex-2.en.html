1 - Introduction and purpose

1.1 Context and genesis of this document

This annex is the result of two successive attempts to connect the Black Hole Universe (BHU) framework with one of the most enigmatic cosmological parameters in contemporary physics: the cosmological constant $\Lambda$. The first attempt, recorded in the previous version of this annex, derived a relation between $\Lambda$ and the mass of the parent black hole from the equality of the temperatures of the two horizons. The second attempt, which corrected the first, derived the same functional relation from a different principle: information conservation and the equality of the horizons' entropies. Both attempts reached the same formal result, but with numerical coefficients differing by an exact factor of four, and both ended up running into the same structural obstacle: the Friedmann equations.

This document integrates the two routes into a single narrative, not as a simple concatenation, but as a progressive argument leading from an attractive idea to a precise understanding of its limits. The purpose is not to present a triumphant derivation, but to show why the derivation is what it is, how far it reaches, and why it cannot go further. This precision about the limits is, in our view, the most valuable contribution of the route.

1.2 The route in synthesis

The starting point is an observation about the structure of the BHU framework that will prove central to everything that follows. The framework proposes that each black hole generates a causally independent child universe. The boundary between parent and child is, from the parent, the black hole's event horizon; from the child, in its asymptotic state, the cosmological horizon of de Sitter space. Both boundaries admit a thermodynamic description: a temperature and an entropy associated with their geometry. That a single physical boundary should have two coherent thermodynamic descriptions, one from each side, is the starting point of the whole derivation.

The first path we explored consisted of equating the temperatures of the two descriptions. The Hawking temperature of the black hole and the Gibbons-Hawking temperature of the de Sitter horizon admit an equality that produces a relation between $\Lambda$ and the mass $M$ of the parent black hole:

$$\Lambda _{tèrmica}=\frac{3c^{4}}{16G^{2}M^{2}}$$

The relation is formally elegant and contains no free parameters. Numerical verification gives a result close to the observed value of $\Lambda$, which made it promising. However, the path has an internal tension: equating temperatures presupposes thermal equilibrium between the two sides of the boundary, and the BHU framework explicitly denies that equilibrium, because the temporal axes of parent and child are orthogonal and there is no instantaneous thermal flux between them.

The second path replaces the equality of temperatures with an equality of entropies. Information conservation across the rupture, which the framework adopts for independent reasons, implies that total information is preserved, and since entropy is the measure of information, the entropies of the two horizons must coincide. This condition is compatible with the structure of the framework because it presupposes no thermal equilibrium: it presupposes only information conservation. The result is:

$$\Lambda _{entròpica}=\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}M^{2}}$$

The functional form is the same; the numerical coefficient differs by a factor of four. This difference is not an error: it is the quantitative signature of the contrary singularity duality, and its interpretation is one of the conceptually richest parts of this document.

The third step was checking the epistemological status of the entropic relation. This is where the wall appears. The Friedmann equations algebraically bind $\Lambda$ to $H_{0}$, $\rho$, and curvature. Any measurable global cosmological quantity — the mass of the observable universe, the density parameter, the age of the universe, the horizon size — contains $\Lambda$ through that constraint. Therefore, deriving $\Lambda$ from any of those quantities is not an independent prediction: it is recovering $\Lambda$ from magnitudes that already contain it. The relation $\Lambda \propto 1/M^{2}$ does not predict the value of $\Lambda$; it expresses the critical flatness condition, and it is consistent with the observed value because our universe is nearly flat.

1.3 Why the route has value

That the relation is not an independent prediction does not make it useless. It has three genuine contributions, developed throughout this document.

The first is that the derivation is correct. The relation $\Lambda =3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)$ follows from well-grounded premises (horizon thermodynamics, the uniqueness of the causal boundary, information conservation) and contains no errors. What had to be revised was not the calculation but its interpretation.

The second is that the factor of four reveals a structure. The difference between the thermal and entropic routes is the square of the factor of two in the surface gravity of the two horizons, which in turn reflects the duality between the black hole (event horizon, energy increasing with radius) and the de Sitter horizon (cosmological horizon, energy decreasing with radius). The factor of four is therefore the signature of the contrary singularity duality expressed in quantitative terms.

The third is that the understanding of the wall is precise. Knowing that $\Lambda$ cannot be predicted within $\Lambda$CDM, and knowing the structural reason why (the Friedmann constraints), is an understanding that situates the relation clearly within the current theoretical landscape. When the final part presents the three routes that could make the relation predictive (modified gravity, holography with an independent microphysical scale, cosmological selection), the diagnosis is precise enough to know exactly which element of each route would break the constraint.

1.4 Structure of the document

The document progresses in seven parts. Parts 2 and 3 establish the thermodynamic foundations and present the thermal derivation, identifying its contribution and its internal tension. Part 4 develops the entropic derivation and analyses the factor of four. Part 5 presents the Friedmann wall and demonstrates the circularity step by step. Part 6 collects the exploratory routes we attempted in order to find a mass independent of $\Lambda$, and explains why they all fail for the same underlying reason. Part 7 closes the document with the open problems and the possible ways out.

The document assumes familiarity with the basic concepts of general relativity and standard cosmology set out in the main theoretical annex.

2 - Thermodynamic foundations of horizons

2.1 Introduction: why horizons emit radiation

One of the most surprising discoveries of twentieth-century physics is that causal horizons emit thermal radiation. An observer confined to one side of a horizon detects a bath of particles at a temperature determined by the geometry of the horizon. This is not a property of the horizon's contents but of the causal structure of spacetime: the mere existence of a horizon limiting what an observer can see is enough to generate a thermal distribution of particles.

This section establishes the quantitative expressions for the temperatures and entropies associated with the two types of horizon relevant to the framework: the event horizon of a Schwarzschild black hole and the cosmological horizon of de Sitter space. Both will be treated as particular cases of a more general thermodynamic structure governing every kind of causal horizon: surface gravity.

2.2 The Hawking temperature

In 1974, Stephen Hawking showed that a black hole in empty space is not, from the quantum point of view, a completely black object. Quantum field theory in curved spacetime predicts that a black hole's horizon emits a thermal spectrum of particles, as if it were a black body at a temperature proportional to its surface gravity.

For a Schwarzschild black hole of mass $M$, the radius of the event horizon is

$$r_{s}=\frac{2GM}{c^{2}}$$

The temperature associated with this horizon, the Hawking temperature, is

$$T_{BH}=\frac{\hbar c^{3}}{8\pi GMk_{B}}$$

where $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $G$ the gravitational constant, $k_{B}$ the Boltzmann constant, and $c$ the speed of light. In terms of the radius $r_{s}$, this expression is written more compactly as:

$$T_{BH}=\frac{\hbar c}{4\pi k_{B} r_{s}}$$

The relationship between temperature and mass is inverse: a more massive black hole is colder, and a black hole losing mass through evaporation heats up progressively. For a solar-mass black hole, the Hawking temperature is of the order of $10^{-7}$ K, utterly negligible compared with the temperature of the microwave background. The Hawking effect is observable in principle, but for astrophysical black holes it is practically undetectable with current technology.

The robustness of this result is worth noting: the Hawking temperature is obtained by three independent routes (canonical quantization in curved spacetime, the Unruh effect for accelerated observers, and field-theory methods on Killing curves), all in agreement. It depends on no specific model of the black hole's interior nor on the nature of the collapsed matter: it is a universal property of the horizon's geometry.

2.3 The Gibbons-Hawking temperature

In 1977, Gibbons and Hawking showed that the cosmological horizon of de Sitter space has an analogous property. An observer in a universe undergoing accelerated expansion, dominated by a cosmological constant $\Lambda >0$, cannot send signals to sufficiently distant regions: accelerated expansion drags distant regions to superluminal recession velocities, and there is a causal horizon limiting what the observer can influence. This de Sitter horizon, like a black hole's horizon, emits a thermal spectrum.

The radius of the de Sitter horizon, for a homogeneous and isotropic universe with cosmological constant $\Lambda$, is

$$r_{dS}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{\Lambda }}=\frac{c}{H}$$

where $H=c\sqrt{\Lambda /3}$ is the constant Hubble parameter of pure de Sitter space. The temperature associated with this horizon is

$$T_{dS}=\frac{\hbar H}{2\pi k_{B}}=\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi k_{B} r_{dS}}$$

Note the structural difference with respect to the Hawking temperature: the de Sitter horizon temperature carries a factor of $2\pi$ in the denominator, whereas Hawking's carries $4\pi$. This factor-of-two difference will be fundamental to everything that follows.

For our present universe, the Gibbons-Hawking temperature is around $10^{-30}$ K, completely inaccessible experimentally. But as a property of the causal structure of spacetime it is as real as the Hawking temperature.

2.4 Surface gravity and the general formula

The Hawking temperature and the Gibbons-Hawking temperature are not two independent results: they are particular cases of a general formula binding the temperature of any causal horizon to its surface gravity.

The surface gravity $\kappa$ of a horizon is, intuitively, the gravitational force per unit mass at the horizon, as measured by a distant observer. For a Schwarzschild horizon, the surface gravity is

$$\kappa _{BH}=\frac{c^{2}}{2r_{s}}=\frac{c^{4}}{4GM}$$

For the de Sitter horizon, the surface gravity is

$$\kappa _{dS}=\frac{c^{2}}{r_{dS}}=c\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda }{3}}=Hc$$

The general relationship between temperature and surface gravity is

$$T=\frac{\hbar \kappa }{2\pi k_{B}c}$$

Verification: substituting $\kappa _{BH}$ into this formula gives

$$T_{BH}=\frac{\hbar }{2\pi k_{B}c}\cdot \frac{c^{2}}{2r_{s}}=\frac{\hbar c}{4\pi k_{B}r_{s}}$$

which coincides exactly with the expression from section 2.2. Substituting $\kappa _{dS}$:

$$T_{dS}=\frac{\hbar }{2\pi k_{B}c}\cdot \frac{c^{2}}{r_{dS}}=\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi k_{B}r_{dS}}$$

which coincides with the expression from section 2.3. The general formula therefore unifies the two temperatures under a single principle: what determines a horizon's temperature is its surface gravity, not the specific nature of the system generating it.

The factor of two between the surface gravities of the two horizons reflects a deep asymmetry: for a black hole, $\kappa \propto 1/r_{s}$ (gravity decreases as the black hole grows), whereas for the de Sitter horizon, $\kappa \propto 1/r_{dS}$ with the sign of the physical effects inverted (the cosmological constant is a source of expansion, not contraction). This inversion is the thermodynamic manifestation of the contrary singularity duality that the BHU framework identifies as its central structure.

2.5 Bekenstein-Hawking entropy

Jacob Bekenstein proposed in 1973 that black holes must have an entropy proportional to the area of their event horizon, and Hawking confirmed this hypothesis in 1974 with the derivation of the temperature. The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy formula is

$$S_{BH}=\frac{k_{B} A}{4 l_{P}^{2}}$$

where $A=4\pi r_{s}^{2}$ is the area of the horizon and $l_{P}^{2}=\hbar G/c^{3}$ is the square of the Planck length. Substituting:

$$S_{BH}=\frac{k_{B}}{4l_{P}^{2}}\cdot 4\pi r_{s}^{2}=\frac{\pi k_{B}c^{3}r_{s}^{2}}{\hbar G}=\frac{4\pi k_{B}GM^{2}}{\hbar c}$$

The entropy is proportional to the square of the mass, or equivalently to the square of the radius. It grows with mass: a more massive black hole has more entropy. This relation $S\propto M^{2}$ contrasts with the relation $T\propto 1/M$: when a black hole gains mass, its entropy increases but its temperature falls. It behaves thermodynamically in the opposite way to most ordinary physical systems, where temperature and internal energy grow together.

The same relation applies to the de Sitter horizon. The Gibbons-Hawking entropy of the cosmological horizon is

$$S_{dS}=\frac{k_{B} A_{dS}}{4 l_{P}^{2}}$$
$$A_{dS}=4\pi r_{dS}^{2}=12\pi /\Lambda $$
$$S_{dS}=\frac{k_{B} c^{3}}{4\hbar G}\cdot \frac{12\pi }{\Lambda }=\frac{3\pi k_{B}c^{3}}{\hbar G\Lambda }$$

The entropy is inversely proportional to $\Lambda$: the larger the cosmological constant, the smaller the de Sitter horizon and the less entropy it contains. For our universe's cosmological constant, the entropy of the de Sitter horizon is around $10^{122}k_{B}$, the largest value a physical system the size of the observable universe can have.

Note the inversion with respect to the black hole case. For the black hole, $S_{BH}\propto M^{2}\propto r_{s}^{2}$: entropy increases with mass and with radius. For the de Sitter horizon, $S_{dS}\propto 1/\Lambda \propto r_{dS}^{2}$: entropy increases as $\Lambda$ decreases, that is, as the horizon grows. In both cases entropy grows with the horizon's area, and that is the universality unifying the two systems. But whereas increasing the black hole's energy increases the radius and therefore the entropy, increasing the cosmological constant decreases the radius and therefore the entropy. Adding energy to the system increases entropy on one side and decreases it on the other: a fundamental asymmetry.

The results of this part can be synthesized in a table showing the relationships between the thermodynamic magnitudes of the two horizons:

First, both horizons obey the same entropic law: $S\propto r^{2}$, that is, entropy is proportional to the horizon's area with the universal coefficient $k_{B}/\left(4l_{P}^{2}\right)$. What distinguishes them is the relationship between energy and radius: for the black hole it is direct and increasing, for the de Sitter horizon it is inverse and decreasing. Both share horizon thermodynamics, but their energetic dynamics are opposite.

This is the thermodynamic duality of horizons, and it is the foundation on which the two derivations that follow (the thermal and the entropic) build their connection between the mass of the parent black hole and the cosmological constant of the child universe.

3 - The thermal derivation and its conceptual tension

3.1 The central idea: one boundary, two temperatures

Part 2 established that the causal boundary between the parent black hole and the child universe admits two thermodynamic descriptions: from the parent, it is a black hole horizon with its Hawking temperature; from the child, in its asymptotic de Sitter state, it is the cosmological horizon with its Gibbons-Hawking temperature. That a single physical surface should have two associated temperatures, one from each side, is a situation that demands a resolution.

The thermal route responds to this situation with a natural condition: if the boundary is unique, the two temperatures must coincide. This condition, $T_{BH}=T_{dS}$, is the starting point of the derivation presented in this part. As will be seen, the condition is formally productive — it generates a relation between $\Lambda$ and $M$ with no free parameters — but it conceals a physical assumption that Part 4 will have to revise.

3.2 The condition of equal temperatures

We impose equality between the Hawking temperature of the parent black hole and the Gibbons-Hawking temperature of the child's de Sitter horizon. From the expressions derived in section 2.4:

$$T_{BH}=\frac{\hbar c}{4\pi k_{B}r_{s}},  T_{dS}=\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi k_{B}r_{dS}}$$
$$T_{BH}=T_{dS}$$
$$\frac{\hbar c}{4\pi k_{B}r_{s}}=\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi k_{B}r_{dS}}$$
$$\hbar c/\left(2\pi k_{B}\right)$$
$$\frac{1}{2r_{s}}=\frac{1}{r_{dS}}$$

The relationship between the two radii resulting from the equality of temperatures is:

$$r_{dS}=2r_{s}$$

The child's de Sitter horizon has twice the radius of the parent's Schwarzschild horizon. The factor of two comes directly from the difference between the $4\pi$ and $2\pi$ factors of the two temperatures, which in turn reflects the difference in the surface gravity of the two horizons, as established in section 2.4.

3.3 Derivation of the relation between Λ and M

Once the relation $r_{dS}=2r_{s}$ is established, the connection between the cosmological constant and the black hole mass is obtained by combining the geometric expressions for the two radii.

The Schwarzschild radius as a function of mass is $r_{s}=2GM/c^{2}$. Therefore $r_{dS}=2r_{s}=4GM/c^{2}$. The de Sitter radius as a function of the cosmological constant is $r_{dS}=\sqrt{3/\Lambda }$. Equating:

$$\sqrt{\frac{3}{\Lambda }}=\frac{4GM}{c^{2}}$$

Squaring and solving for $\Lambda$:

$$\frac{3}{\Lambda }=\frac{16G^{2}M^{2}}{c^{4}}$$
$$\Lambda _{tèrmica}=\frac{3c^{4}}{16G^{2}M^{2}}$$

This is the relation obtained by the thermal route. It connects the cosmological constant of the child universe with the mass of the parent black hole through the fundamental constants $G$ and $c$, with no adjustable parameter.

3.4 Numerical verification

The numerical validity of the relation is checked by substituting the observed values of our universe.

$$\Lambda _{obs}\approx 1.11\times 10^{-52} \qquad ^{-2} \qquad M_{obs}=c^{3}/\left(2GH_{0}\right) \qquad H_{0}=67.4 \qquad ^{-1} \qquad ^{-1} \qquad M_{obs}\approx 8.8\times 10^{52}$$

Substituting into the thermal relation:

$$\Lambda _{tèrmica}=\frac{3\times \left(3.00\times 10^{8}\right)^{4}}{16\times \left(6.67\times 10^{-11}\right)^{2}\times \left(8.8\times 10^{52}\right)^{2}}\approx 4.1\times 10^{-53} m^{-2}$$

The thermal relation predicts a value of $\Lambda$ lower than the observed one by a factor of $\Lambda _{obs}/\Lambda _{thermal}\approx 2.7$. Within an order of magnitude, the coincidence is notable: starting from purely thermodynamic reasoning about the framework's causal boundary, one arrives at a value close to the observed one with an error of a factor of three.

However, as will be seen in Part 5, the numerical quality of a result is no guarantee of the correctness of the derivation, and the factor of three conceals a circularity that must be exposed.

3.5 The conceptual tension: why equating temperatures is problematic

The condition $T_{BH}=T_{dS}$ is formally natural (that a single boundary should have a single temperature seems coherent) but it conceals a physical assumption that the BHU framework cannot sustain.

In thermodynamics, equality of temperatures between two systems has a precise meaning: it is the condition of thermal equilibrium. Two systems reach the same temperature when they have exchanged thermal energy until the net flux between them vanishes.

Thermal equilibrium requires the two systems to be in thermal contact, that is, able to exchange heat. Imposing $T_{BH}=T_{dS}$ therefore implies that the parent black hole and the child's de Sitter horizon are in thermal equilibrium or, at minimum, that there is some physical reason why their temperatures must coincide — and the only physically standard reason for temperatures to coincide is equilibration through thermal exchange.

This is where the tension appears. The BHU framework establishes two principles that directly contradict the possibility of thermal equilibrium between parent and child:

The first is the absence of instantaneous thermal flux. The analysis of the relationship between the parent's Hawking radiation and the child's internal evolution (presented in the main theoretical annex) shows that the literal interpretation of Hawking radiation as a flux of particles coming from the child is incompatible with the timescales. The internal processes constituting the child's evolution operate at rates that cannot feed the emission rate the Stefan-Boltzmann formula predicts for the parent. The connection between child and parent is not an instantaneous causal flux but an informational or global correspondence. If there is no instantaneous flow of heat between the two sides of the boundary, there is no equilibration process to justify the equality of temperatures.

The second is the independence of the temporal axes. The framework proposes that the child's temporal axis is orthogonal to the parent's, so that the child's internal history admits no instant-by-instant correspondence with the parent's time. Thermal equilibrium is intrinsically a temporal process: two systems equilibrate over time, and their approach to a common temperature is a dynamic evolution. If the two systems do not share a common temporal axis along which that equilibration can take place, the very notion of thermal equilibrium between them loses its foundation. You cannot thermally equilibrate along a time that is not shared.

The conclusion is that the thermal route imposes a condition of thermal equilibrium that the framework denies by two independent paths. The derivation is formally consistent, but the condition it imposes is not available within the framework.

3.6 The nature of the problem: a condition without justification

It is worth being precise about what has been identified, because the precision determines the appropriate response. The problem is not that the equality $T_{BH}=T_{dS}$ is false — we have no argument that directly refutes it — but that it is not grounded within the framework. The standard physical reason why two temperatures coincide, equilibration through thermal exchange, is not available. And without a grounded reason why they should coincide, the condition is an ad hoc hypothesis that can be imposed for its numerical productivity but does not rest on the framework's principles.

In scientific research, a condition without justification may produce correct or incorrect results, but in no case can it claim the explanatory value of a grounded derivation. The thermal route therefore has the value of an interesting heuristic result, but not that of a rigorous derivation within the BHU framework.

The natural way out is to replace the condition of equal temperatures with a different condition that is grounded in the framework's principles. Part 4 presents that condition and the derivation that follows from it.

4 - The entropic derivation

4.1 The alternative condition: from temperatures to entropies

Part 3 showed that the condition of equal temperatures, despite its numerical productivity, is not grounded within the BHU framework because it presupposes a thermal equilibrium the framework denies. A different condition is therefore needed — one that describes the relationship between the two thermodynamic descriptions of the boundary and is compatible with the framework's principles.

The condition we propose is the equality of the entropies of the two horizons: $S_{BH}=S_{dS}$

This condition is fundamentally different from the equality of temperatures. Equality of temperatures is a condition of dynamic equilibrium: it presupposes that two systems exchange energy until they reach the same temperature. Equality of entropies is a condition of conservation: it presupposes that total information is preserved when passing from one description to the other. The first demands thermal contact; the second demands only that information be conserved.

The BHU framework adopts information conservation as a working principle for independent reasons, and Part 4.2 articulates them. Here we anticipate the conclusion: the entropic route is the correct one for the framework not because it is numerically better (in fact its result differs slightly from the observed value) but because its central condition is coherent with the framework's principles, whereas the thermal route's condition is not.

4.2 Premises of the derivation

The entropic derivation rests on four premises. We classify them explicitly by their epistemological status, because the soundness of the whole argument depends on the soundness of each one.

Premise 1 — Horizon entropy is proportional to area (established physics):

For any causal horizon, the associated entropy is $S=k_{B}A/\left(4l_{P}^{2}\right)$, where $A$ is the area of the horizon. This relation is an established result of black hole thermodynamics, demonstrated by Bekenstein and Hawking, and shown to be valid for cosmological horizons by Gibbons and Hawking. It is not a hypothesis of the framework; it is established physics that the framework inherits.

Premise 2 - The child's asymptotic state is one of maximum entropy (established within the framework):

The BHU framework establishes that the child universe, on reaching its asymptotic de Sitter state, has derealized all matter: all content has become causally disconnected through accelerated expansion, and there is no physical process that can increase entropy any further. The entropy of the child universe at terminal de Sitter is therefore the maximum value it can reach, and it equals the Gibbons-Hawking entropy of its cosmological horizon. The argument that this state is one of maximum entropy requires no additional hypothesis: if there is no physical process capable of increasing entropy, entropy has reached its terminal value.

Premise 3 — The causal boundary is unique (consequence of the framework):

What from the parent is described as the black hole's event horizon and what from the child is described as the de Sitter cosmological horizon are two descriptions of a single physical surface: the causal boundary separating the two domains. These are not two surfaces coinciding by chance but a single boundary seen from two sides. The uniqueness of the boundary is a direct consequence of the structure of the BHU framework, which proposes that the child is born inside the parent's black hole: the boundary that closes the black hole from outside is the same one that closes the child universe from inside.

Premise 4 — Information is conserved (central hypothesis of the framework):

The framework adopts the principle of information conservation: quantum information is neither created nor destroyed in the parent-child cycle, and the global state of the system evolves unitarily. Applied to the cycle, this premise asserts that the information entering the parent's black hole, which constitutes the child universe, is preserved throughout the child's entire evolution. Since entropy is the measure of information, information conservation implies entropy conservation when passing from one description to the other.

We must be honest about the status of this fourth premise. It is not established physics but a hypothesis the framework adopts. There are respected positions in theoretical physics holding that information can be genuinely lost in processes involving horizons. The framework does not demonstrate that those positions are incorrect; it sets them aside as a working hypothesis. The hypothesis is nevertheless motivated: rejecting information conservation would lead to the impossibility of any physical connection between parent and child, eliminating the framework's explanatory capacity.

4.3 The central condition derived from the premises

From the four premises, the derivation's condition follows directly. Premise 2 establishes that the child's maximum entropy, reached at terminal de Sitter, is the Gibbons-Hawking entropy of its cosmological horizon. Premise 3 establishes that this horizon is the same boundary that, from the parent, is the black hole's horizon. Premise 4 establishes that information is conserved when passing from one description to the other, and since entropy measures information, the entropy must be the same from both sides.

Combining the three premises:

$$S_{BH}\left(pare\right)=S_{dS}\left(fill al de Sitter terminal\right)$$

The entropy the parent attributes to the black hole must coincide with the entropy the child reaches in its terminal state. Both count the same total information, measured from the two sides of a single causal boundary, and information is conserved.

4.4 The derivation step by step

Step 1: entropy of the parent black hole. The Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of a Schwarzschild black hole of mass $M$ is, from section 2.5:

$$S_{BH}=\frac{4\pi k_{B}GM^{2}}{\hbar c}$$

Step 2: entropy of the child's de Sitter horizon. The Gibbons-Hawking entropy of the de Sitter horizon is, from section 2.5:

$$S_{dS}=\frac{3\pi k_{B}c^{3}}{\hbar G\Lambda }$$

Step 3: equating. Imposing $S_{BH}=S_{dS}$:

$$\frac{4\pi k_{B}GM^{2}}{\hbar c}=\frac{3\pi k_{B}c^{3}}{\hbar G\Lambda }$$

Step 4: simplification. Cancelling the common factors $\pi$, $k_{B}$ and $\hbar$:

$$\frac{4GM^{2}}{c}=\frac{3c^{3}}{G\Lambda }$$

Step 5: solving for $\Lambda$. We multiply both sides by $\Lambda$ and divide by $4GM^{2}/c$:

$$\Lambda =\frac{3c^{3}}{G}\cdot \frac{c}{4GM^{2}}=\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}M^{2}}$$

The result of the entropic derivation is:

$$\Lambda _{entròpica}=\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}M^{2}}$$

4.5 The factor of four between the two routes

$$\Lambda _{tèrmica}=3c^{4}/\left(16G^{2}M^{2}\right)$$
$$\frac{\Lambda _{entròpica}}{\Lambda _{tèrmica}}=\frac{3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)}{3c^{4}/\left(16G^{2}M^{2}\right)}=4$$

The factor of four is neither a calculation error nor an ambiguity in the constants. It comes directly from the difference between the conditions imposed by the two routes. The thermal route imposes $r_{dS}=2r_{s}$, whereas the entropic route imposes $r_{dS}=r_{s}$.

The entropic route equates entropies, which scale with the square of the radii: $S\propto r^{2}$. The equality $S_{BH}=S_{dS}$ is therefore equivalent to the equality of the areas, which is equivalent to the equality of the radii: $r_{dS}=r_{s}$.

The thermal route equates temperatures, which for the Schwarzschild horizon scale as $T_{BH}\propto 1/r_{s}$ and for the de Sitter horizon as $T_{dS}\propto 1/r_{dS}$, but with numerical factors $4\pi$ and $2\pi$ respectively. The equality $T_{BH}=T_{dS}$ implies $1/\left(2r_{s}\right)=1/r_{dS}$, that is $r_{dS}=2r_{s}$.

Since $\Lambda =3/r_{dS}^{2}$, the factor-of-two difference in the radius translates into a factor-of-four difference in the cosmological constant:

$$\Lambda \propto \frac{1}{r_{dS}^{2}}: r_{dS}^{\left(ent\right)}=r_{s}, r_{dS}^{\left(tèrm\right)}=2r_{s} \Rightarrow  \frac{\Lambda _{ent}}{\Lambda _{tèrm}}=\frac{\left(2r_{s}\right)^{2}}{r_{s}^{2}}=4$$

The factor of four is therefore the quantitative signature of the difference between equating entropies and equating temperatures. As seen in section 2.4, the difference between the $4\pi$ and $2\pi$ factors in the temperatures reflects that the surface gravity of the Schwarzschild horizon, at equal radius, is half that of the de Sitter horizon. The factor of four in $\Lambda$ is the square of the factor of two in surface gravity.

4.6 The contrary singularity duality

The factor of four is not an isolated technical detail: it connects with a deep structural property of the relationship between the two horizons that the BHU framework calls the contrary singularity duality.

The two horizons share the same entropic law ($S\propto A/4l_{P}^{2}$) but exhibit energy-radius relations of opposite sign. For the black hole, increasing the mass increases the radius and the entropy: $r_{s}\propto M$, $S_{BH}\propto M^{2}$. The more energy the black hole contains, the larger its horizon. For the de Sitter horizon, increasing the cosmological constant decreases the radius and the entropy: $r_{dS}\propto \Lambda ^{-1/2}$, $S_{dS}\propto \Lambda ^{-1}$. The greater the vacuum energy density, the smaller the cosmological horizon.

The two systems respond in opposite ways to the addition of energy: the black hole grows, the de Sitter horizon contracts. Both share horizon thermodynamics but their energetic dynamics point in opposite directions. This is the duality.

The factor of four is the quantitative manifestation of this duality in the context of the derivation. When entropies are equated, the two horizons are identified in the magnitude they share: the entropic law $S\propto r^{2}$. The result, $r_{dS}=r_{s}$, is the condition that the radii coincide, and it implies that the temperatures will not coincide (because the surface gravity is different). When temperatures are equated, one attempts to identify them in the magnitude where they differ through the duality (surface gravity), and the result, $r_{dS}=2r_{s}$, is the witness of that difference.

In short: the factor of four is not an obstacle but a result. It says that equating the shared magnitude (entropy) and equating the opposed magnitude (temperature, via surface gravity) give geometric conditions differing exactly by the square of the duality factor.

4.7 Reading the result and properties of the relation

The relation $\Lambda =3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)$ has several properties worth highlighting before turning to the analysis of its epistemological status.

Absence of free parameters. The relation contains no adjustable parameter. Once the mass of the parent black hole is fixed, the cosmological constant of the child is completely determined by the fundamental constants $G$ and $c$. The coefficient $3/4$ comes directly from the geometry of the two horizons.

Inversion: The relation can be rewritten as

$$M=\frac{c^{2}}{G}\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\Lambda }}=\frac{\sqrt{3} c^{2}}{2G\sqrt{\Lambda }}$$

which gives the mass of the parent black hole as a function of the child's cosmological constant. Applied to our universe, it allows the mass that the black hole which generated us should have to be calculated. With

$$\Lambda _{obs}\approx 1.11\times 10^{-52} \qquad ^{-2}$$
$$M_{pred}=\frac{\sqrt{3}\times \left(3.00\times 10^{8}\right)^{2}}{2\times 6.67\times 10^{-11}\times \sqrt{1.11\times 10^{-52}}}\approx 1.17\times 10^{53} kg\approx 5.9\times 10^{22}M_{⊙}$$

Direction of the inference. The relation connects an external property of the black hole (the mass, observable from the parent) with an internal property of the universe it contains (the cosmological constant, observable from the child). Given the parent's mass, one can calculate the child's $\Lambda$. Given the child's $\Lambda$, one can infer the parent's mass. This is the kind of parent-child connection the framework seeks: a property of the child determined by a property of the parent.

4.8 Numerical verification of the entropic route

$$M_{obs}\approx 8.8\times 10^{52}$$
$$\Lambda _{entròpica}=\frac{3\times \left(3.00\times 10^{8}\right)^{4}}{4\times \left(6.67\times 10^{-11}\right)^{2}\times \left(8.8\times 10^{52}\right)^{2}}\approx 1.64\times 10^{-52} m^{-2}$$

The ratio between the predicted and observed values is:

$$\frac{\Lambda _{entròpica}}{\Lambda _{obs}}=\frac{1.64\times 10^{-52}}{1.11\times 10^{-52}}\approx 1.48$$

The entropic route overestimates the observed value by a factor of approximately $1.5$, that is, an error of $48%$. Compared with the factor of $2.7$ of the thermal route, the entropic route is numerically better, and a factor-$1.5$ discrepancy in $\Lambda$ corresponds to a factor-$\sqrt{1.5}\approx 1.22$ discrepancy in the mass, which is $22%$.

The $22%$ discrepancy in the mass is in fact notable when placed in the right context. The mass of the observable universe is an uncertain quantity: it depends on $H_{0}$, which current measurements do not quite pin down (the Hubble tension), and on the matter density, which includes contributions from dark matter that are not directly observable. A $22%$ uncertainty in the mass of the universe is perfectly plausible. Moreover, the mass of $5.9\times 10^{22}M_{⊙}$ that the relation predicts for the parent black hole coincides remarkably with the mass Gaztañaga (2022) obtains from independent dynamical considerations ($\sim 5.6\times 10^{22}M_{⊙}$).

However, as will be shown in Part 5, neither the numerical proximity nor the coincidence with Gaztañaga guarantees that the relation is a genuine prediction. The factor of $1.5$ is honest and informationally rich, but the question of whether it constitutes a prediction or a consistency requires an analysis that goes beyond numerical comparison.

5 - The Friedmann wall: why the relation is consistency and not prediction

5.1 The first reading and its appeal

The relation $\Lambda =3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)$ looks, on a first reading, like a prediction. If we know the mass of the parent black hole $M$, we can directly calculate the child's cosmological constant $\Lambda$. If we identify $M$ with the mass of the observable universe and calculate the resulting value of $\Lambda$, we obtain a number close to the observed value. The appeal of this result is understandable: starting from a purely thermodynamic argument about the causal boundary of a theoretical framework, one arrives at a value for the cosmological constant without adjusting any parameter.

However, this first reading is incorrect, and its incorrectness is not a technical detail but a structural property of the standard cosmological model. Understanding why the relation is not an independent prediction is, paradoxically, one of the most valuable results of the exploration documented in this annex. Knowing precisely why an argument fails is as informative as knowing why an argument works.

5.2 The Friedmann equations as an algebraic constraint

The standard cosmological model $\Lambda$CDM describes the evolution of a homogeneous and isotropic universe from the Friedmann equations, which result from applying the Einstein equations to the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric.

The first Friedmann equation, for a flat universe ($k=0$), is:

$$H^{2}=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho +\frac{\Lambda c^{2}}{3}$$

where $H=\dot{a}/a$ is the Hubble parameter, $\rho$ is the total matter-energy density and $\Lambda$ is the cosmological constant. Today, the relative densities are expressed in terms of the density parameters:

$$\Omega _{m}=\frac{\rho _{m,0}}{\rho _{c,0}},  \Omega _{\Lambda }=\frac{\Lambda c^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}},  \rho _{c,0}=\frac{3H_{0}^{2}}{8\pi G}$$
$$\Omega _{m}+\Omega _{\Lambda }=1 \qquad \Omega _{\Lambda }\approx 0.69 \qquad H_{0}\approx 67.4 \qquad ^{-1} \qquad ^{-1}$$

The key observation is that $\Lambda$ appears explicitly in the Friedmann equation as one of the terms determining $H$. Any global cosmological quantity — the critical density $\rho _{c}$, the Hubble parameter $H_{0}$, the mass of the observable universe — is calculated from the Friedmann equation and therefore contains $\Lambda$ implicitly. Let us see this explicitly for the mass of the observable universe.

5.3 The mass of the universe contains Λ

The mass of the observable universe is obtained by multiplying the critical density by the volume of the Hubble sphere:

$$M_{obs}=\frac{4}{3}\pi r_{H}^{3}\cdot \rho _{c,0}=\frac{4}{3}\pi \left(\frac{c}{H_{0}}\right)^{3}\cdot \frac{3H_{0}^{2}}{8\pi G}=\frac{c^{3}}{2GH_{0}}$$

So far, $M_{obs}$ appears independent of $\Lambda$: it depends only on $H_{0}$ and the fundamental constants. But $H_{0}$ is not independent of $\Lambda$. From the definition of $\Omega _{\Lambda }$:

$$\Omega _{\Lambda }=\frac{\Lambda c^{2}}{3H_{0}^{2}} \Rightarrow  H_{0}^{2}=\frac{\Lambda c^{2}}{3\Omega _{\Lambda }}$$

Therefore:

$$M_{obs}=\frac{c^{3}}{2G}\cdot \frac{1}{H_{0}}=\frac{c^{3}}{2G}\cdot \sqrt{\frac{3\Omega _{\Lambda }}{\Lambda c^{2}}}=\frac{c^{2}}{2G}\sqrt{\frac{3\Omega _{\Lambda }}{\Lambda }}$$
$$\Lambda \qquad H_{0} \qquad \Omega _{\Lambda } \qquad M=M_{obs} \qquad \Lambda =3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)$$
$$\Lambda =\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}}\cdot \frac{1}{M_{obs}^{2}}=\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}}\cdot \frac{4G^{2}\Lambda }{c^{4}\cdot 3\Omega _{\Lambda }}=\frac{\Lambda }{\Omega _{\Lambda }}$$

This is not a prediction: it is $\Omega _{\Lambda }=1$, a statement that would be incorrect ($\Omega _{\Lambda }\approx 0.69$), or equivalently $\Lambda =\Lambda /\Omega _{\Lambda }$, which, unless $\Omega _{\Lambda }=1$, is a contradiction. What has happened is that by substituting $M_{obs}$ — which already contains $\Lambda$ — into the relation, we have recovered $\Lambda$ circularly.

5.4 The circularity: general demonstration

The preceding result is not specific to the way we calculated $M_{obs}$. It is a structural property of the Friedmann equations. Any global cosmological quantity $Q$ that can be written in terms of $H_{0}$, $\rho _{c,0}$, $\Omega _{m}$ or $\Omega _{\Lambda }$ contains $\Lambda$ implicitly, because all these quantities are algebraically bound to $\Lambda$ by the Friedmann equation.

Let us formulate this precisely. The Friedmann equation binds $\Lambda$, $H_{0}$ and $\Omega _{m}$ in a single relation:

$$H_{0}^{2}\left(1-\Omega _{m}\right)=\frac{\Lambda c^{2}}{3}$$

(for a flat universe). Therefore, knowing two of the three parameters ${H_{0},\Omega _{m},\Lambda }$ determines the third by algebra. If $H_{0}$ and $\Omega _{m}$ are known, $\Lambda$ is known. If $H_{0}$ and $\Lambda$ are known, $\Omega _{m}$ is known. Any quantity expressed in terms of two or more of these parameters implicitly contains the third.

The consequence for the entropic relation is the following. The relation $\Lambda =3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)$ connects $\Lambda$ with $M$. To check numerically whether it is correct, a measurement of $M$ independent of $\Lambda$ is needed. But $M$, for any cosmologically relevant definition (mass of the observable universe, mass within the de Sitter horizon, Gaztañaga's mass), is expressed in terms of $H_{0}$ and $\Omega _{\Lambda }$, which already contain $\Lambda$. Substituting $M$ into the relation is therefore substituting an expression that already contains $\Lambda$, and recovering $\Lambda$ is not a prediction but a verification that the Friedmann equations are internally consistent.

5.5 Gaztañaga's mass as a candidate, and why it does not break the circularity

Once the circularity is identified, the natural question arises: is there a mass that is genuinely independent of $\Lambda$ and could serve to test the relation as a prediction?

Enrique Gaztañaga (2022) has calculated, in the context of his own black hole cosmology framework, the mass of the parent collapse that would have generated our universe, obtaining a value of $M\approx 5\times 10^{22}M_{⊙}$. As seen in section 4.7, this value coincides remarkably with what the entropic relation predicts as the parent black hole's mass for our observed value of $\Lambda$. The coincidence, with a relative error of less than 10%, appears to validate the relation.

$$\Omega _{\Lambda }\simeq 0.7 \qquad H_{0}\simeq 70 \qquad ^{-1} \qquad ^{-1} \qquad r_{S}=r_{\Lambda }=r_{H}\left(a\to \infty \right) \qquad r_{\Lambda }=\sqrt{3/\Lambda } \qquad r_{\Lambda }=\sqrt{3/\Lambda } \qquad r_{S}=2GM/c^{2}$$
$$\frac{2GM_{Gaz}}{c^{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{\Lambda }} \Rightarrow  M_{Gaz}=\frac{c^{2}}{2G}\sqrt{\frac{3}{\Lambda }}$$

Gaztañaga's mass is exactly proportional to $\Lambda ^{-1/2}$: it contains $\Lambda$ explicitly. Substituting into the entropic relation:

$$\Lambda _{pred}=\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}M_{Gaz}^{2}}=\frac{3c^{4}}{4G^{2}}\cdot \frac{4G^{2}\Lambda }{c^{4}\cdot 3}=\Lambda $$

The substitution gives $\Lambda =\Lambda$: an algebraic identity. The numerical coincidence between Gaztañaga's mass and the mass the entropic relation predicts is not an independent validation but a consequence of both using the same geometric condition $r_{s}=r_{dS}$, expressed in apparently different but algebraically equivalent ways.

5.6 The correct status of the relation

The preceding analysis allows the exact status of the relation $\Lambda =3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)$ within the BHU framework to be established.

The relation is consistent with $\Lambda$CDM. Any universe satisfying the Friedmann equations will satisfy the relation if the mass is expressed in appropriate terms. But consistency is not prediction: that a relation is satisfied by all models of a class does not mean it has predictive content about the parameters of that class.

The relation expresses the critical flatness condition. If the mass is isolated from the relation:

$$M=\frac{\sqrt{3} c^{2}}{2G\sqrt{\Lambda }}$$

this is exactly the value of the critical mass $m_{c}\sim c^{2}/\left(G\sqrt{\Lambda }\right)$ that Popławski (2010) identifies as the threshold between a child universe that expands forever and one that recollapses. The entropic relation does not predict the value of $\Lambda$; it expresses the condition that the child universe sits at the critical threshold between eternal expansion and recollapse, that is, that it is nearly flat. The relation coincides numerically with the observed value because our universe is nearly flat ($\Omega \approx 1$), not because it predicts the specific value of $\Lambda$.

The relation is therefore a structural consistency of the BHU framework with $\Lambda$CDM: a result showing that the framework's principles (information conservation, uniqueness of the causal boundary, horizon thermodynamics) produce a relation that integrates coherently with the standard cosmological model. But the numerical value of $\Lambda$ cannot be extracted from the framework without previously knowing some parameter that already contains it.

5.7 To break the circularity: what would be needed

Identifying the wall makes it possible to formulate precisely what would be needed to overcome it. The circularity lies in the fact that every cosmologically relevant mass contains $\Lambda$ via the Friedmann equations. To break it, a mass $M$ derived from a source genuinely independent of $\Lambda$ would be needed: a microphysical scale, a dynamic condition of the collapse, or a mechanism that did not pass through the Friedmann constraint.

A scale of this kind exists in Popławski's framework: the Cartan density, $\rho _{C}=m_{n}^{2}c^{4}/\left(G\hbar ^{2}\right)$, where $m_{n}$ is the neutron mass. This density comes from nuclear physics, is independent of $\Lambda$, and fixes the density at the bounce of the collapsed matter. The mass of the universe at Popławski's bounce, $m_{⋆}=\left(128/27\right)^{1/2}M_{BH}^{2}m_{n}/m_{Pl}^{2}$, is independent of $\Lambda$.

However, the bridge between the bounce mass $m_{⋆}$ and the present mass of the universe requires integrating the cosmological evolution from the bounce to the present, which passes through the Friedmann equations and reintroduces $\Lambda$. Popławski adjusts the reduction factor between $m_{⋆}$ and the present mass by hand; he does not derive it. The wall cannot be circumvented from within $\Lambda$CDM: overcoming it requires stepping out into a richer framework, as discussed in Part 7.

6 - The exploratory routes and why they fail

6.1 The problem formulated and the strategy of exploration

Part 5 established that the relation $\Lambda =3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)$ is a structural consistency, not an independent prediction, because every cosmologically relevant mass contains $\Lambda$ via the Friedmann equations. The natural question that follows is whether there is any route to constructing a mass independent of $\Lambda$ that would turn the relation into a genuine prediction.

We have systematically explored several strategies. All have run into the same obstacle, for reasons that, once identified, converge on a single conclusion. Documenting these attempts is valuable not because any of them works, but because the pattern of their failures illuminates the structure of the problem and makes it possible to state precisely what would be needed to solve it.

6.2 - Route 1: Gaztañaga's mass as an independent candidate

The first candidate for an independent mass is the one Gaztañaga (2022) obtains for his black hole cosmology. His framework proposes that the universe is born from the collapse of a region of the parent above the critical mass, and calculates the mass of that collapse from dynamical considerations. The resulting value, $M_{Gaz}\approx 5\times 10^{22}M_{⊙}$, coincides with what our entropic relation predicts as the parent's mass for the observed value of $\Lambda$.

As demonstrated in section 5.5, however, Gaztañaga's mass is proportional to $\Lambda ^{-1/2}$: his derivation uses the condition $r_{s}=r_{\Lambda }$, which is algebraically equivalent to our condition $S_{BH}=S_{dS}$. The numerical coincidence is not an independent cross-validation: it is the manifestation that both frameworks express the same geometric condition through apparently different formulations.

This conclusion can be verified directly. Gaztañaga's paper (2022) explicitly states that the mass is obtained “for $\Omega _{\Lambda }\simeq 0.7$ and $H_{0}\simeq 70$ km s$^{-1}$ Mpc$^{-1}$”, confirming that the mass depends on $\Omega _{\Lambda }$, which contains $\Lambda$. Gaztañaga's route does not break the circularity.

6.3 - Route 2: the bounce mass in Popławski and the Cartan density

Nikolaos Popławski (2010, 2012), in the context of Einstein-Cartan cosmology, provides a derivation of the child universe's mass from a scale genuinely independent of $\Lambda$. The Cartan density,

$$\rho _{C}=\frac{m_{n}^{2}c^{4}}{G\hbar ^{2}}\approx 4.9\times 10^{54} kg m^{-3}$$

where $m_{n}$ is the neutron mass, comes from nuclear physics and contains no $\Lambda$. The mass of the universe at the bounce is:

$$m_{⋆}=\left(\frac{128}{27}\right)^{1/2}\frac{M_{BH}^{2} m_{n}}{m_{Pl}^{2}}$$

This mass depends on the parent black hole's mass $M_{BH}$ and on fundamental constants, with no reference to $\Lambda$. It is the independent piece the entropic route would need.

The problem is the bridge. The bounce mass $m_{⋆}$ in Popławski is around $10^{32}M_{⊙}$ for a stellar black hole, six orders of magnitude above the mass of our universe. To connect $m_{⋆}$ with the present mass one would have to model the mass reduction during the relativistic expansion following the bounce, which requires integrating the cosmological evolution and reintroduces the Friedmann equations.

Popławski does not derive that bridge: he adjusts the six-order-of-magnitude reduction factor by hand so that the final mass matches that of our universe. The Cartan scale is independent of $\Lambda$, but the bridge from it to the present mass is not.

6.4 - Route 3: flatness as a route to an independent mass

The flatness of our universe ($\Omega \approx 1$) is another possible route. If the parent black hole's mass could be calculated from the condition that the child universe be flat — without using $\Lambda$ — one would have an independent mass. We have explored several variants of this idea.

Variant 3a: size at Popławski's bounce.

$$M_{BH} \qquad a_{⋆}=\left(27/128\right)^{1/6}\left(2G/c^{2}\right)\left(M_{BH}^{2}m_{Pl}^{2}/m_{n}\right)^{1/3}$$
$$\left|\Omega -1\right|_{⋆}=\frac{3c^{2}}{8\pi G\rho _{⋆}a_{⋆}^{2}}$$

For the critical mass ($M\approx 5.6\times 10^{22}M_{⊙}$), the numerical calculation gives $\left|\Omega -1\right|_{⋆}\approx 5\times 10^{-60}$. Evolving that value to the present gives $\left|\Omega -1\right|_{0}\approx 5\times 10^{-18}$: extraordinarily flat, far more so than observed. For the observed flatness value ($\left|\Omega -1\right|_{0}\lesssim 0.01$) one would need a black hole mass far below the critical one, around $10^{10}M_{⊙}$ (a supermassive black hole mass), completely different from the mass the entropic relation requires.

Flatness and the critical mass of the entropic relation therefore demand masses differing by twelve orders of magnitude: they are not linked.

Variant 3b: Planck density.

Replacing the Cartan density with the Planck density makes the flatness calculation worse. Flatness at the bounce scales as $\left|\Omega -1\right|_{0}\propto \rho _{⋆}^{1/6}$: a higher bounce density implies a less flat universe.

Going to Planck density gives a present-day flatness of order $\left|\Omega -1\right|_{0}\sim 10^{-12}$, which is even less consistent with the flatness problem than the result at Cartan density.

Variant 3c: inverted rupture density.

If instead of assuming the density and calculating the flatness one inverts the problem (what bounce density would give the observed flatness for the critical mass?), the result is a density of $\sim 10^{-26}$ kg m$^{-3}$, comparable to the present density of the universe. A “rupture” density of that order makes no physical sense as either a Planck or a Cartan regime: it is the present density, not a collapse density.

The conclusion is that flatness and the cosmological constant demand incompatible conditions for the parent black hole's mass. There is no physically motivated bounce density that produces both the observed flatness and the critical mass of the entropic relation. The two conditions belong to independent physical registers.

6.5 - Route 4: the rupture, the absence of geometry, and flatness by default

A fourth exploration approached the question from the nature of the rupture. The BHU framework does not propose a continuous bounce (like Popławski or LQC) but a genuine rupture of the manifold: the parent's geometry is interrupted and the child's begins with no geometric continuity. At the rupture, curvature is not defined — there is no manifold to support it. One might think that if curvature is undefined at the rupture point, the child emerges with no inherited curvature and is therefore flat by default.

The argument fails for a specific reason. That the parent's curvature is not transmitted does not mean the child is born without curvature. The child emerges with all the mass-energy content of the parent black hole, and the child's curvature is determined, via the Einstein equations, by the relationship between that energy density and the initial expansion rate. Curvature is not a property that is inherited: it is a property the child's content generates. A child full of dense mass-energy is not a flat child.

The precise formulation is the following. Flatness ($\Omega =1$) is the exact tuning between energy density and expansion rate: $\rho =\rho _{c}=3H^{2}/\left(8\pi G\right)$. The rupture conserves energy but not geometry; the child's initial expansion rate $H$ is not determined by the rupture. If energy is conserved but the expansion rate is free, the relationship between them — which is what fixes flatness — is free. Flatness is not a default value in the absence of information: it is a specific and finely tuned value among all the possible ones. The absence of geometric inheritance does not select flatness; it leaves curvature undetermined.

Genuine rupture and flatness are therefore structurally incompatible for the very reason that makes the framework distinctive: the rupture destroys exactly the geometry-content tuning that flatness would require.

6.6 - Route 5: the mass from the size of the CMB

A final route explored whether the size of the universe measurable from the patterns of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) allowed $\Lambda$ to be derived independently. The position of the CMB's acoustic peaks contains information about the geometry of the universe and allows the curvature parameter $\Omega _{k}$ to be measured. Combining $\Omega _{k}$ with the matter density, one can derive $\Omega _{\Lambda }=1-\Omega _{m}-\Omega _{k}$, and from there $\Lambda$.

The circularity is the same. Extracting the parameter $\Omega _{k}$ from the CMB patterns is done by fitting a cosmological model that contains $\Lambda$ as a parameter. The position of the peaks depends on the distance to the last scattering surface, which depends on the entire expansion history of the universe, which depends on $\Lambda$. Therefore $\Omega _{k}$ measured from the CMB already contains $\Lambda$ implicitly. Deriving $\Lambda$ from $\Omega _{k}$ is, once again, recovering $\Lambda$ from a quantity that already contains it.

6.7 The common pattern: every route meets the same wall

The five routes explored — Gaztañaga's mass, Popławski's mass, flatness for various bounce densities, the rupture and flatness by default, and the size of the CMB — all fail for the same structural reason, manifested in different ways:

Any global cosmological quantity (mass of the universe, density parameter, horizon radius, age of the universe) is calculated from the Friedmann equations, and the Friedmann equations algebraically bind $\Lambda$ to $H_{0}$, $\rho$ and $k$. It is not possible to measure a cosmologically relevant quantity without $\Lambda$ appearing in it implicitly. Every route starting from a cosmological quantity ends up recovering $\Lambda$ circularly.

The routes that try to avoid cosmology — the Cartan scale, flatness through the bounce density — either fail to connect with the present mass without passing through Friedmann, or demand masses incompatible with the entropic relation. There is no known microphysical scale that, connected with the present mass without passing through the Friedmann equations, produces the value of $\Lambda$.

The conclusion, established in Part 5 and confirmed by this part's exploration, is robust: the circularity is structural, not contingent. It is not an obstacle that ingenuity or creativity could circumvent within the framework of $\Lambda$CDM. Overcoming it requires a new physical element not subject to the Friedmann constraints — that is, stepping outside $\Lambda$CDM.

7 - Open problems, possible ways out, and closing

7.1 The value of open problems

A theoretical framework that identifies its limitations precisely is more trustworthy than one that hides them. Parts 5 and 6 established that the relation $\Lambda =3c^{4}/\left(4G^{2}M^{2}\right)$ does not constitute an independent prediction of the value of the cosmological constant within $\Lambda$CDM. Accepting that conclusion rigorously is what makes it possible to formulate the open problems precisely, and the precision of the open problems determines the framework's usefulness for future research.

This part presents the three main problems the document has identified, the possible routes for addressing each, and two contributions of the framework that do constitute genuine results, defensible independently of how the $\Lambda$ problem is resolved.

7.2 Problem 1: the predictability of Λ outside ΛCDM

The BHU framework produces the relation $\Lambda \propto 1/M^{2}$, which is a structural consistency with $\Lambda$CDM. To turn it into a genuine prediction would require a mass $M$ derived from a source independent of $\Lambda$, and a law of evolution connecting that mass with cosmologically observable quantities without passing through the Friedmann constraints. Within $\Lambda$CDM, neither condition can be satisfied simultaneously.

There are three families of modification to the framework that could, in principle, overcome this limitation.

Route 1: modified gravity. In modified gravity theories, the Einstein-Hilbert action $\int R\sqrt{-g} d^{4}x$ is replaced by $\int f\left(R\right)\sqrt{-g} d^{4}x$, where $f$ is a function of the Ricci scalar. In these theories, cosmic acceleration does not come from an independent constant $\Lambda$ but from the form of $f\left(R\right)$, and the effective value of $\Lambda$ is bound to the function's parameters. If the form of $f\left(R\right)$ were motivated by independent principles (symmetries, renormalizability), the effective value of $\Lambda$ could be determined without passing through the standard Friedmann constraint. The problem, as Odintsov and collaborators note in the recent literature, is that predicting the value of $\Lambda$ shifts to the problem of the form of $f\left(R\right)$, which demands its own justification.

Route 2: holography with a microphysical scale. The framework's entropic derivation already belongs conceptually to the family of holographic arguments: the condition $S_{BH}=S_{dS}$ is a condition on horizon areas, and horizon areas are the holographic measure of information. The missing step is a length scale independent of $\Lambda$ that supplies the relevant radius. Popławski's Cartan density provides an energy scale independent of $\Lambda$, but the bridge between that scale and the present mass is not derived. If a law were established connecting the Cartan density with the present mass without adjusting parameters by hand, the missing piece would be in place. Of the three routes, this seems the closest to the spirit of the BHU framework.

Route 3: cosmological selection. The BHU framework has affinities with Smolin's cosmological natural selection (1992): universes reproduce via black holes, and the constants vary slightly between generations. In a selection scenario, the value of $\Lambda$ need not be derived from an equation: what must be shown is why selection favours the observed value. Smolin argues that selection maximizes black hole production, which casts $\Lambda$ in terms of star-formation density and gravitational collapse. Weinberg (1987) shows, by a different anthropic argument, that $\Lambda$ cannot be much larger than the observed value without preventing structure formation. Neither argument predicts the exact value of $\Lambda$, but they situate its order of magnitude.

7.3 Problem 2: flatness and the rupture

The flatness of our universe ($\Omega \approx 1$) is one of the established results any cosmological framework must be able to integrate. The BHU framework, as seen in section 6.5, does not explain it from first principles, and the reason is structural: a genuine rupture of the manifold preserves content (mass-energy) but not geometry. The precise tuning between energy and expansion rate that flatness requires ($\rho =3H^{2}/8\pi G$) cannot be inherited if geometry does not cross the rupture.

This incompatibility is a direct consequence of the framework's distinctive feature. The rupture is what differentiates the BHU framework from continuous-bounce frameworks (Popławski, Gaztañaga, LQC): in a continuous bounce, geometry does cross and the tuning could survive. The BHU framework pays the price of the rupture with the impossibility of inheriting flatness.

Two mechanisms could integrate flatness into the framework without abandoning the rupture. The first is cosmic inflation inside the child: inflation, if it operates in the child in its early phase, flattens any initial curvature as it does in the standard model. The BHU framework is perfectly compatible with inflation inside the child; it simply neither generates nor motivates it. The second is a selection mechanism: if black holes with masses below the critical mass generate children that recollapse quickly (and generate neither black holes nor descendants), selection will favour masses close to the critical threshold, and children at the critical threshold will be approximately flat. This route connects directly with cosmological natural selection.

7.4 Genuine result 1: the horizon problem and unitary origin

Unlike the flatness problem, there is another cosmological problem that the BHU framework does address genuinely and without additional ingredients: the horizon problem.

The horizon problem is the following. In the standard cosmological model, opposite regions of the sky that could never have been in causal contact (because the distance between them exceeds the distance light could have travelled since the Big Bang) show the same microwave background temperature to a precision of one part in a hundred thousand. Why are they so perfectly homogeneous if they could never have exchanged information?

Inflation solves this problem by exponentially expanding a causally connected region to the dimensions of the observable universe. The BHU framework offers a different and, in certain respects, more natural resolution. If the entire content of the child universe comes from a single black hole in the parent, and if the state at the moment of rupture is a minimum-entropy state — a unitary, highly coherent state with all content in a single causal domain — then the child's homogeneity is a direct consequence of the common origin. Everything comes from the same state, and therefore everything has been causally connected. Distant regions need not have exchanged information during expansion: they had the same information from the start because both come from the same origin.

This resolution is robust because it depends neither on flatness nor on $\Lambda$: the unitary origin guarantees the child's homogeneity (and isotropy) independently of its curvature and of the value of its cosmological constant.

7.5 Genuine result 2: low initial gravitational entropy and Weyl erasure

The second genuine result connects the rupture with the problem of initial gravitational entropy, which Penrose has identified as one of the fundamental questions of cosmology: why did the early universe have such low gravitational entropy?

In Penrose's formulation, gravitational entropy is measured by the Weyl tensor: a homogeneous and isotropic universe has a vanishing Weyl tensor, and therefore minimal gravitational entropy from its own foliation. A universe full of irregularities — black holes, galaxy clusters, any structure — has a non-vanishing Weyl tensor and high gravitational entropy. Penrose's Weyl hypothesis postulates that universes begin with a vanishing Weyl tensor for reasons current physics does not explain.

The BHU framework offers an argument in support of this hypothesis. The rupture of the manifold breaks the geometric connection between parent and child. The parent's free curvature — encoded in the Weyl tensor — is a geometric property that does not cross the rupture. Local curvature forced by matter (the Ricci tensor) can be taken to cross along with the mass-energy. The result would be that the child is born with a vanishing Weyl tensor (no free curvature inherited from the parent) but a non-vanishing Ricci tensor (the curvature forced by its matter density). It is born, in Penrose's terminology, with low initial gravitational entropy.

This argument is not a rigorous derivation — formalizing the rupture in terms of topological cobordisms, and of how each component of the curvature tensor does or does not cross the discontinuity, would require a mathematical treatment beyond this framework — but it identifies a conceptually coherent mechanism. The child is born with low gravitational entropy not because physics demands a special initial condition, but because the rupture erases the parent's free curvature as a consequence of the geometric discontinuity. This is potentially one of the framework's most valuable conceptual contributions to theoretical cosmology.

7.7 Rupture versus continuous bounce: the pending structural decision

The BHU framework, as presented in the main theoretical annex, adopts a genuine rupture of the manifold as the mechanism of the child's genesis. This choice is what distinguishes the framework from continuous-bounce frameworks (Popławski, Gaztañaga, LQC) and is what gives meaning to the orthogonal temporal axes, to derealization, and to a genuine causal boundary.

However, the rupture has a precise cost that this annex has documented: the impossibility of inheriting flatness and the impossibility of deriving $\Lambda$ without additional help. The continuous bounce has the symmetric cost: it loses the distinction and the orthogonal axes, but potentially allows flatness to be inherited and $\Lambda$ to be connected with the mass via geometric continuity. Neither option resolves all the problems simultaneously.

This is a decision the framework will have to take explicitly and defend. The rupture is what makes the framework distinctive and what connects with the Weyl-erasure result; but it demands that flatness be explained by an additional mechanism. The bounce is what connects with Popławski and Gaztañaga; but it would abandon the radical nature of temporal orthogonality and the genuine causal boundary. Neither is superior to the other in the abstract: the preference will depend on the future results each path generates.

7.8 Closing

The route documented in this annex has led from an attractive observation — that the causal boundary between parent and child admits two thermodynamic descriptions that can be connected — to a precise understanding of its limits.

The thermal derivation produces a consistent relation but rests on a condition (thermal equilibrium) that the framework cannot sustain. The entropic derivation improves the physical grounding and is the one the framework adopts. The two differ by a factor of four which, far from being a problem, is the quantitative signature of the duality between the two horizons. The resulting relation is consistent, free of adjustable parameters, and invertible: it gives the mass of the parent black hole as a function of the child's value of $\Lambda$, with a notable coincidence with Gaztañaga's mass.

However, the relation does not predict $\Lambda$ independently. The Friedmann equations bind $\Lambda$ to every cosmologically measurable quantity, and any mass identifiable from cosmological observation contains $\Lambda$ implicitly. Overcoming that wall requires new physics outside $\Lambda$CDM, by any of the three routes identified in section 7.2.

On balance, the framework contributes two genuine things that do not require overcoming the wall: the resolution of the horizon problem via unitary origin, and the argument for low initial gravitational entropy via the erasure of the Weyl tensor at the rupture. Both results belong to the class of conceptually coherent proposals that, in theoretical physics, constitute serious contributions even in the absence of direct observational verification.

The cosmological constant remains an open problem in physics. The BHU framework does not solve it from first principles. But it offers a new angle on why it is difficult, and on what properties a theory that solved it would need to have.
